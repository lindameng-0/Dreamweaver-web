title: A Client Who Does Not Dream
vigil: VIGIL I — THE COMMISSION
folio: 19

The house stood at the end of Widow's Lane where the streetlamps gave up and the stars took over the shift. It was a narrow house, shuttered like a held breath, and the woman who opened the door did so before he knocked, which told him she had been listening for footsteps the way other people listen for thunder.

Sayo Kurose was not what the letter had let him imagine. He had pictured someone faded, worn translucent by whatever was wrong with her nights. Instead she had the stillness of deep water and a gaze that arrived slightly before the rest of her attention, and she looked at his raised hands — he had lifted them without thinking, framing her against the hall light — with the patience of a woman who has been measured by doctors and priests and found, by every instrument, perfectly well.

“You're the weaver,” she said. “You're younger than the last three.” Then, because he was still standing in her doorway with his hands up like a photographer or a burglar: “Come in. I'll make tea, and you can tell me why this time will also not work.”

The tea was good. The house was wrong. It took him the length of one cup to name it: there was no hum. Every house in the city hums at night, low and almost friendly, the sound of its sleepers' dreaming being drawn up and away like smoke up a flue. Her house was silent the way a snapped string is silent. The Loom did not reach here. The Loom, he realised, with a feeling like a stair missing in the dark, did not know this address existed.

“Every night at the same hour,” she said, “I am in a theatre.” She said it the way people recite an address. “The seats are full of people made of the dark between stage lights. Someone is performing my grief. He gets it exactly right, which should be impossible, because I have never told anyone what it is. And they applaud — softly, like snow — and I wake with my hands sore from not clapping. Doctors say I have not slept. The Loom Office says I have not dreamed. I have done both, Mr. Amano, every night for eleven years.”

The rule book was clear as winter. A weaver mends what the Loom holds; what the Loom has never held, no licence covers. There was no thread here to pick up, no seam to unstitch, nothing but a silent house and a level-eyed woman and a nightmare that existed somewhere no lawful needle had ever gone. The correct answer was an apology and a referral and the long walk home.

Riku took out his contract book. He was aware, distantly, of the lacquered box in his head, and of how light it had felt lately, and of the exact sound of applause like snow, which he had heard before — once — in a dream that had not belonged to a client. “I'll need eleven nights,” he said. “One for each year. And I'll need you to stop making tea this good, or I'll draw the work out just to keep coming back.” It was not a joke. Neither of them laughed. She signed.
