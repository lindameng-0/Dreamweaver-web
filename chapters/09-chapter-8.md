vigil: VIGIL I — THE COMMISSION

His hand was over his eyes.

He did not know how long it had been there. His palm fit the bridge of his nose and his fingers curved across his brow and the heel of his hand pressed into the bone above his left eye and the pressure was the only thing holding the shape of his face together. His breathing was loud.

The ink was drying on his hands. He could feel it tightening across his knuckles, in the creases of his fingers, pulling the skin into something stiff and foreign. The room smelled of it. Ink and torn paper.

Under his breathing he heard something.

Footsteps. They came down the corridor with an evenness that was almost musical, each step falling at the same interval as the last, and beneath them another sound. Something like feathers.

He did not move his hand from his eyes.

The door did not open. No knock came.

They moved away down the corridor, the sound thinning until the corridor swallowed it. Luca sat with his hand over his eyes.

The ink pulled at his skin. The ceiling was there above him, the fourteen cracks, the plaster, the same. The pages were still on the floor, still on the walls, still everywhere. Nothing in the room had changed. The light through the window was the color it was.

He sat for a while longer.

Then he stood. Slowly. And crossed to the door. His hand found the handle. His thumb settled on the curve of the metal.

His hand did not turn the handle immediately. His hand stayed where it was.

At last, he turned the handle.

And the corridor was empty. The light from the overhead lamps ran down the stone in both directions, pooling at even intervals, and the floor was clean and the walls were clean and nothing in the passage held any evidence that someone had been standing here.

He stood in the doorway. He could step back. Close the door, return to the bed or the floor or the desk.

He walked. He did not choose a direction. His body chose for him, turning left out of the door because the left was where the footsteps had gone, though the footsteps were long past hearing and the corridor gave him nothing to follow. He walked the way a person walks through an unfamiliar building after a long illness, one hand almost touching the wall.

He passed a courtyard. He passed a door propped open with a stone, leading to a garden he did not enter. He passed a woman carrying a basket who glanced at him and looked away and he did not see her face. His head was down. His hair hung forward, unwashed, the ink still in it from where his hands had been.

He did not know how long he walked. The corridors became streets. The overhead lamps became shop fronts, closed for the evening, their awnings drawn. The cobblestones under his feet were uneven and he felt each one. The air smelled different out here. Bread from somewhere, though the bakeries were shut. Woodsmoke. The flowering vines that grew along every wall in Auralis, the ones he had smelled from the window, the scent he had shut out and opened and shut out.

He stopped.

He was standing in front of a door. The door was wood, dark, with a brass handle that had been touched enough times to wear the finish off one side. Light came through the cracks around the frame. Warm light. The kind that moved, that came from candles or oil lamps and not from the steady glow of overhead fixtures. From inside, the low murmur of voices. Not distinct, not words he could separate, just the sound of people existing in the same room.

He stood there. He could feel his pulse in his wrists.

He pushed the door.

The warmth hit him first. The air inside was different from the corridor and different from the street. It smelled of oak and alcohol and the accumulated warmth of conversation. The light was low and amber and it came from fixtures along the walls that cast everything in a color that softened edges. Tables occupied most of the floor. The bar itself ran along the far wall. A long stretch of dark wood with bottles on shelves behind it, glasses hanging from a rack overhead, catching the candle light in small repeated sparks.

Behind the bar, a man was pouring something from a bottle into a glass. He tipped the bottle with his wrist and the liquid caught the light and he set the glass on the counter in front of a woman who was talking to him. His sleeves were pushed to his elbows and his wings were folded close against his back. The feathers caught the candlelight and the gold in them was the same gold as the liquor in the bottles on the shelf behind him.

He was listening, leaning forward on one forearm, his other hand around a glass he was drying without looking at it. The cloth moved in slow circles over the rim. He said something and the woman laughed. Eli laughed too, like whatever she had said was the most interesting thing anyone had said in a long time.

Eli did not look toward the door.

Luca looked away. He found a table in the corner, against the wall, where the amber light was thinnest. He sat with his back to the room. The chair was wood and the table was wood and the surface had the scuffs and rings of a table that had held a hundred glasses. He put his hands on it. The wood was smooth from use, warm from the room.

The bar went on.

The voices around him were a single continuous fabric, woven from threads he could not separate. A woman at the table nearest the door was telling a story with her hands. Two men at the bar were arguing about something, their voices rising and falling in a rhythm that had nothing to do with anger. Someone behind him laughed.

There were too many people in this room. That was the thought, though it was not a thought so much as a sensation, a pressure against the surfaces of him, the way water presses against a glass held under a tap. He sat in his corner. His hands were on the table. The ink was still on them, dark in the creases, under the nails. The candle on his table was small and nearly spent and the wax had pooled on the wood around it in a pale circle.

He looked at Eli.

Eli was pouring. A long, steady arc from the bottle to the glass. He set the bottle down. He slid the glass across the bar to a woman who caught it with two fingers and said something Luca could not hear. Eli smiled.

He moved down the bar. He wiped the surface with a cloth that appeared in his hand and disappeared again in the same motion. He leaned in to hear something a man was saying, and whatever the man was saying brought Eli's head down and his shoulders in and the smile left. He listened. He said something back, quiet, and the man's shoulders dropped an inch and Eli poured him another drink and set it before him and the man held the glass in both hands and Eli went on wiping the counter and did not look at the man again but stayed close, within reach, the cloth moving in its slow circles, and the man drank and the two of them existed in that proximity for a while and then the man finished his drink and put money on the counter and left.

Luca turned his gaze back down when Eli came out from behind the bar to clear tables. He moved through the room without resistance, filling every space that opened. He started at the one nearest the door. He collected glasses. He wiped surfaces. He asked a table something and a woman there answered and he nodded and moved on.

He moved from table to table. Closer. Each one bringing him nearer to the corner where Luca sat, and Luca could track the approach by the sound of the cloth on wood, the small clink of glasses being gathered, the brief exchanges. The words were ordinary. They were the words of a man doing a job. *What are you having today. Can I get you another.*

He reached Luca's table.

Luca's eyes went to the wood grain in front of him. The surface was scarred and stained, years of rings from the bottoms of glasses overlapping each other in a pattern that meant nothing and could not be read.

"What are you looking for?"

Eli's voice. Close.

Luca did not answer. He did not look up. His hands stayed on the table, the ink on them dark against the wood, and the candle between them was guttering, the wax was still pooling.

Two seconds. Three.

Eli did not ask again. There was a motion at the edge of Luca's vision. A small nod, perhaps. The kind a waiter gives to a table that is not ready to order. The cloth moved once across the far edge of Luca's table, a brief wipe that went around his hands without touching them. Then Eli's footsteps, moving away, overlapped by the sound of a glass being set on the counter by the many voices.

Luca sat.

The candle on his table went out. The wax had reached the wick and drowned it and the small thread of smoke that rose from it was the only proof that there had been a flame at all. The corner was darker now. Luca sat in the dark at the edge of the room and the room went on without him and the going on was not unkind. It was simply what the room did. It held the people in it and the people talked and drank and laughed and one by one they began to leave.

The woman near the door left first. Then the two men at the bar. Then the table behind him, chairs scraping, coats being pulled on, a voice saying something about the weather. The room thinned. The layers peeled away.

The last table stood. Settled their glasses on the bar. Said something to Eli that Luca could not hear. The door opened and closed behind them and the draft it made passed through the room and was gone.

Quiet.

Eli began the work of closing. He moved behind the bar. The sound of bottles being placed in their positions. The cloth, slower now, making long passes across the wood. The tap running briefly. A glass being rinsed. He turned off the lamp above the bar and the room dimmed. He turned off the one above the door and the room dimmed further. The only light left was the two candles still burning on tables nobody had sat at, and the faint glow from the street that came through the cracks around the frame.

Eli came out from behind the bar. He moved through the room. He collected the last glasses. He straightened the chairs. He reached Luca's table and the cloth came down on the surface and moved across it the way it had moved across every other surface, even and unhurried, going around Luca's forearms, around the dead candle, around the pale circle of wax, and continuing to the far edge and back, as though this were a table that happened to have a person at it, nothing more.

"We're closing up," Eli said. The voice was the voice from before. The hotel desk at midnight. The last announcement before the doors shut. "Have a good night."

He did not say Luca's name. He did not say anything that the sentence did not contain. He stepped back from the table. He turned.

And as he turned, his hand came down and set something on the table's edge. Metal on wood. A single clear note.

A key.

Eli's footsteps crossed the floor. The door opened. The street noise came in for a moment, the distant sound of the city winding itself down, and then the door closed and the noise was gone and the footsteps and feathers were gone, leaving only luca and the key on the edge of his table where Eli had left it.

He only sat there silently, along with the amber smell of liquor, the warmth left behind by others. A glass on a far table still had some water in it, catching the last of the candlelight.

The key was on the table.

Luca did not touch it. He only looked. It was brass, or something close to it. Dark with use. The teeth were simple. It was the kind of key that opened one door and no other, and the door it opened was the door Eli had walked through.

He sat for a long time.

He put his head down on his arms.

The wood of the table was cool against his forearm. The ink on his hands was dry and hard and it pulled when he curled his fingers.

The key was under his hand.

He slept. Not well, and not for long, and in a posture that was not sleep so much as surrender. A person who had stood for as long as he could and sat for as long as he could and was now simply not conscious, in a room that smelled of wood and alcohol.

The last lights went out in the windows across the street.

Somewhere past the rooftops, past the residential quarter, past the eastern wall, the Garden waited in whatever way a place that might not exist waits for a person who might never come.

Inside the bar, the dark was gentle. The bottles stood their watch. The glasses hung in their rows. The cloth lay folded over the tap. And at the table in the corner, the prophet slept with a key under his hand and the ink of the future drying on his hands, and the room held him the way rooms hold anyone, which is to say, without judgment, and without promise, and for only as long as they stay.

The light was different when it came back.

Later, or the next day, or some measure of time that had passed without announcing itself. The street outside had gone through its cycle and returned. The thin line beneath the door had been dark and then grey and then gold and then dark again and now it was the amber of early evening, the hour when the lamps in the street came on and the windows across the way began to glow.

Luca was behind the bar.

The wood was in front of him now, the long stretch of it, the grain running left to right under his hands. The bottles were behind him. The glasses hung above him.

He was making something. The bottle in his hand was dark glass and the label was in the script he could not read and the pour was steady. He watched the liquid fill the glass to the line he had seen Eli fill it to, the exact line, and his wrist turned and the pour stopped and the bottle went back. His other hand reached for a second bottle without looking and found it where it was and added what it added.

He set the glass on the bar. He looked at it. The color was close. The smell was close. He poured it out and started again.

The door opened, and a man entered. He was in his mid-thirties. He came in the way people come into bars, already halfway to sitting before the door closed behind him, his eyes adjusting to the low light, finding the bar, finding the person behind it. He paused for half a step. His eyes moved once to the door, as though checking he had walked into the right place. Then he sat down on the nearest stool and put his hands on the counter.

"Whatever's easy," the man said.

Luca's hands moved. The dark bottle. The pour. The second bottle. The glass slid across the bar and the man caught it.

The man drank. He set the glass down. He looked at the bottles behind Luca and then at the room and then at some point between the two。

"You know what I keep thinking about," the man said.

Luca was wiping the bar. The cloth moved in long passes, left to right, the wrist turning at the end to fold the cloth against the grain.

"There's this route I take through the market. Every morning. Same stalls, same turns. And there's a corner where the sun comes through between two buildings for about ten minutes, right around seven. Just this one patch of ground. I keep thinking I should stop there one morning. Just stand in it."

Luca did not respond. His hand continued its path across the wood.

"Never do, though. There’s always somewhere to be." The man took another drink. He looked at the glass. "It'll be gone by winter. The angle changes."

The cloth stopped. Luca reached for the bottle and refilled the man's glass without speaking.

The man watched him for a moment. Something about the quality of the silence behind the bar seemed to settle him. He drank slower after that. He said a few more things. A neighbor who had promised to fix a gutter and never did. A dog that slept in the alley behind his building and how it was there every morning without fail, curled in the same spot, and he had started leaving bread for it. Small things. The kind of things a person says to the air above a glass when the day is over and the air is warm and there is someone across the counter who will not interrupt.

Luca said nothing. He stood behind the bar with the cloth in his hand and the bottles at his back. His eyes rested on the man when the man spoke and moved away when the man paused and came back when the man continued, and the rhythm of that was steady and unhurried and gave the impression of a person who was listening carefully to something being said at a great distance. The man talked. He said more than he had probably planned to say.

Then the door opened.

The sound it made was the same sound it had made every other time it opened that evening. The draft that came through was the same draft. The street noise, the cobblestones, the distant quality of Auralis winding down for the night.

Eli walked in.

He did not pause at the door. He crossed the room, passed behind the tables, and came around the end of the bar the way a person comes around a corner in their own home. Luca's hands stopped on the glass he was drying. His weight shifted. A fraction backward, toward the gap at the far end of the counter where the bar opened onto the floor, the route out, the route back to the other side where customers sat and the person behind the bar was someone else's job.

Eli picked up a glass from the rack. He filled it from the tap and set it in front of a woman at the counter who had been waiting. Eli said something. She answered. He set another glass under the tap. Eli did not look at him. Eli was looking at the tap, at the glass, at the woman, at the row of bottles behind him that needed straightening, and each thing he looked at received the full weight of his attention and none of that weight landed anywhere near Luca.

Eli said something to a man at the far end of the bar. The man said something back. Eli laughed, and the sound was the same sound from the night before, unhurried, warm, filling its small corner and leaving. The evening continued. The cloth passed from one end of the bar to the other. The glasses came down and went up. The bottles turned and returned.

At some point the room had thinned enough that the silences between conversations grew longer than the conversations themselves. Eli was wiping down the far end of the bar. Luca was rinsing a glass. The water ran over his hands and the ink that was still under his nails tinted the water for a moment and then the water was clear again and the glass was clean and he set it on the rack and reached for the next one.

The last table left. Eli walked them to the door. He said something Luca did not hear and the people laughed and the door opened and closed.

Eli came back behind the bar. He put the bottles in their places. Luca dried the last glass and hung it and the rack was full and the stems all faced the same direction. Eli turned off the lamp above the door.

The room was dark except for the street light at the edges of the door. And then with a creak of the door, Eli had left.

Luca walked out from behind the bar. He crossed the room. The chairs were pushed in and the tables were wiped and everything was where it would need to be when the door opened again.

He let himself out. The key turned the lock behind him. The street was cool and empty and the cobblestones under his feet were the cobblestones he had walked the night before.

The door to his room was unlocked. He opened it.

The pages were still on the floor. The ink was still on the walls. The notebook's carcass was where he had thrown it, above the bed, the plaster cracked beneath it. The room was exactly as he had left it. The wreckage waiting in the purple light with the patience of wreckage, which does not clean itself up, and does not need to, and will be there in the morning.

He stepped inside and closed the door behind him.

Eli came in without knocking, which he never did, and set two glasses down on the table by the door.

"It's the fourth."

Luca did not look up. He waited to see whether the sentence intended to explain itself.

"There's a meteor shower on the fourth," Eli said. "Every year. Or there was, at some point, when somebody was in a position to check."

The glasses had come from downstairs. Luca knew that because there were only four of that shape behind the bar and two of them had a chip out of the rim, and one of the two on the table had the chip, which meant Eli had taken whatever was nearest and not looked. There was a bottle under his arm. He had not mentioned the bottle.

Luca sat back.

"Nobody in this place has seen a star, not to mention meteors. There's a barrier above our heads and there's smoke over the barrier and there's four hundred kilometers of dust over that, and on the clearest night anyone has ever had, the sky was grey."

Eli's wings shifted behind him, a small resettling, the way a person shifts weight from one foot to the other. They were larger this week. They had been larger every week since the barrier work started and nobody had said anything about it, which Luca took to mean that either nobody had noticed or everybody had.

"You would know," Luca said.

"I do."

"Then what exactly are you planning to do about it."

"Go up and look."

There was a version of the evening in which Luca said no. He could see it clearly, the way he could see most things, the door closing and the map still open and the hours between then and morning proceeding in the order they had already been assigned. He stood up.

The stairs to the roof were narrow and turning. Eli went first. Three flights of that, and then the low door at the top that stuck, and then the air.

It was warm. It was always warm. The roof came away from the door in two long pitches of terracotta, tile on tile in overlapping rows, and the tiles were still holding the day in them. Luca went up the slope on his hands once and then walked the rest, and sat down on the ridge with a leg on either side of it.

The city lay out below in its sprawl of pale walls and red roofs going down toward the water, lamps still burning in a scattering of windows. Past the last of them the ground rose again and the barrier came down over all of it like a lid set on a pot. Above the lid, cloud. Not weather. Just the permanent grey underside of everything, faintly lit from beneath by the city's own lights thrown back down again.

The tower stood at the north end of the roof where the ridge ran into it. It had been there as long as Luca had been in the bar. Luca did not look up at it, as always.

Eli set the glasses down in the channel between two tiles. He poured, and did not offer, and left one standing where a hand could reach it.

Then he raised his arm and pointed east, at nothing.

"There."

Luca looked east. There was cloud there. There was cloud everywhere, but there was a particular quantity of it in that direction, and Eli was pointing at it with the flat certainty of a man indicating a landmark to a visitor.

"I went up once, some time not too long after the Cataclysm," Eli said. "Straight up through it, higher than I had any business going. There was nothing to breathe by the end and I was very calm about all of it, which in hindsight was the worrying part."

"And?"

"And I have nothing for you." He lowered the arm. "Four years to come up with something and I don't have a single word about it that isn't a lie. Cloud, maybe. Or smoke. Something white, or something that wasn't white at all. I came down and I couldn't have told you which direction I'd come from."

Four years of turning it over and arriving nowhere. Luca had a fair idea of what that was like from the inside, and he was not going to say so.

"So you've never seen a single star either."

"No. But I know where they go."

He stood up off the parapet.

One hand went up, open, and a thread went with it. It left his palm and climbed and kept climbing, thin and gold and perfectly straight, past the roofline and past the height of anything in the district, up into the underside of the cloud where it entered and did not stop and did not come back. Where it ended, something opened. A small round glow at the tip of the thread, no bigger than a coin held at arm's length, the light coming off the endpoint itself and sitting in the grey as though it had been let in through a hole.

Five more went up. Quickly, one after another. And then his hand turned and went across, and a sixth thread ran from one of the lights to the next, and to the next after that, laying the line in between them, and the six loose points closed into a shape.

"That's Lyra," Eli said. "The bright one at the corner is Vega. It was the north star ten thousand years ago and it'll be the north star again in about the same."

He said it the way people said the names of streets in the district they grew up in.

"You know it from something," Luca said.

The hand kept moving. A moment went by, long enough that Luca had begun to file the question under the ones that did not get answered.

"Drawings," Eli said. "Someone drew them out for me when I was small. On paper, because there was nothing to point at. And then made me say them back until I could do it without the paper in front of me."

Another thread went up and stopped and opened, and a second one crossed to meet it, and the shape took one more corner.

"They weren't very good drawings. But I've kept it exactly the way I was given it."

"You were how old."

"Six."

"And you can still set them in the right places, in the right order, without stopping to think about it."

Eli did not turn around, and there was no way to see his face, which was possibly the point and possibly not.

"I've probably lost a few. It's been a long time. She was wrong about a fair amount of it as well. I know that now. Two of the names have never been in any book I've found and one of them I'm fairly sure she made up on the spot because I wouldn't go to bed."

His hand stopped. It stayed up.

"She was a liar about small things and honest about large ones. I've come to think that's the better arrangement."

The gold hung where he had left it. He added nothing and said nothing for a while.

Then the hand moved again, and another shape opened above them.

"That square is part of the harp. Or part of an eagle, depending which century you're asking."

Luca picked up the glass.

He drank about a third of it, which was more than the shape of the evening called for, and set it back down on the tile. It was something out of Eli's own shelf and it had the sweetness that came from a heavy hand with the bitters and it was, by any measure that mattered, not good.

"You've made a very good copy of something you have never seen," Luca said, "for someone who has no way of checking it."

"That's true."

"You could have put anything up there."

"I could have. But then when you finally see it, you'd know I lied. And I would rather you didn't."

Luca reached over and took the bottle. He poured badly, more than he intended, and set it back down on the parapet with the label turned away from him.

"That isn't going to happen."

"Which part?"

"Any of it." He drank. "Nobody standing on this roof tonight is going to die having seen one, and neither will their children, if this place ever has any again."

Eli’s arm did not falter. It went on out toward the east with the same unhurried pace it had had for the last hour.

"That's not true."

"You have to stop with the nonsense—"

"The Garden's is clear."

Luca said nothing. He drank. It had stopped tasting of anything in particular a while ago, which he understood to be the point of the exercise.

"The whole of it, all the way down. It's the clearest sky anyone alive has stood under. The look on your face doesn't quite believe me, and I don't think that will change until you see it with your own eyes."

"If it isn't in the prophecy, it isn't important."

Eli laughed, not unkindly. He had laughed several times over the course of the evening, most likely an ordinary reflexive laugh, though it was impossible to tell when it was not.

"Then am I important?"

Luca did not answer.

Another shape went up. Eli's hand did not hurry.

"You'd better say no. A man in the corridor with the wrong door open. I'll take that. Happily. Provided the rest of it's there."

"You don't get to choose."

"No, but I can tell you which arrangement I'd accept." A beat. "Because the one where I'm standing at the end and that place isn't. I want to know what I was supposed to be doing there. What the point of me was, standing about."

The threads climbed. Somewhere east of them a light in a window went out, and the street below took on a slightly different shade, and nothing else in the world changed.

"When you see it, you'll understand everything I am saying, have said, and will say."

"In that case, I don't think I ever will."

"But you already have. Every time somebody says the name your mind goes somewhere for about a second and comes back."

Luca kept his eyes on the gold. He was aware of the shape of his own face and of the fact that nothing had moved in it, and he was aware that this was very likely the least useful thing about him, because the man was not looking at his face and never had been.

"So what's there."

The silence went on a long time. Long enough for the city to turn over once, a shutter, the sound of the winds a long way off. Luca had answered harder questions than this in front of worse people.

"It's nothing," he said. "It's a room with one window and the rest is imagination."

"How do you know it's nothing?"

"How do I know I made it up." he stared incredulously at Eli.

"The last time something arrived in your head that you hadn't put there, you told me it was a prophecy."

He turned. The wings came around with him and the light of forty-odd small suns was behind him now so that the whole front of him was in shadow, and he looked at Luca with the patience of a man who has all night, who has, by his own arithmetic, considerably more than that.

Luca stared at him for a moment.

"You're being ridiculous," He said.

"I'm only asking. How do you know?"

"Because I'm not a child, Eli.” He set the glass down harder than he meant to and it rang once against the tile. “Because imagination is imagination and prophecy is prophecy and one of them is something I can see with my eyes shut because I wanted to and the other has never once cared what I wanted."

"But if—"

"You want me to tell you that I saw the Garden in a vision.” He said it slowly, “That it came to me the way the rest of it comes.”

Eli said nothing.

“Don't do this. You're smarter than this."

"I did hope that, yes." Mildly, without any apparent damage. "But does the difference truly matter?"

"Does it—" Luca stopped. He started again, quiet. "One of them's real, Eli. That isn't a small distinction. That's the whole of it. Because if it isn't real then it's just you, and wanting something doesn't make it—"

"Does it matter which."

He had already turned back to the sky.

"You've spent multiple hours looking at these. Suppose I've got half of it wrong and the real ones sit nowhere near where I've put them. Would tonight have been worth less?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I'd wake up again tomorrow."

A pause.

"Yes," Eli said. "You would."

Luca put the glass down on the parapet. It had gone warm in his hand a while ago and he had gone on drinking from it anyway.

"You can stand under something all night and the moment you lower your hands it's gone, and the sky's the same sky it was, and it will be that sky when everyone on this roof is dead." He heard how flat it came out and had no interest in correcting it. "I'd rather not learn to love the version one person can take down."

Eli said nothing for a while.

Another shape went up, west, unhurried.

"That's fair. I won't bring it up again."

The silence after that was not a comfortable one and neither of them made any attempt to do anything about it. Luca watched a thread go up and open, and thought about how many times in his life he had been given a thing on the condition that he already know how it ended, and how this was the first occasion he could remember where somebody had gone to the trouble of building the thing anyway.

"Though I must say, the garden." Eli's hand moved out another few degrees. "It's the one place a person ought to see once. Whatever else happens to them."

A long silence.

A long time passed. The gold spread past the roofline and out over the street below, and then over the next street after it, and the undersides of the balconies down there took on a faint warmth they had not been built to receive. The light did not reach the ground. It stopped some distance above it, the way lamplight does in fog, so that the whole district lay in a shallow bath of it with the dark left underneath.

Luca had lost track of how long the arm had been up. That was the part he could not get past. Not the shapes, not the accuracy of them, not the dead woman's bad drawings preserved in a six-year-old's head for two decades and then hung out over a city. The arm. A man could hold his arm up for a certain length of time and Eli had passed that length some hours ago and had not once let it drop, and there was no audience up here, and nobody was counting, and he had simply gone on doing it.

"The sky there," Luca said.

"Mm?"

"Is it these."

Eli paused.

"No."

"Then what is it."

"It's real."

Somewhere below them a door opened onto the street and a woman's voice came up, saying something to somebody, and a second voice answered her, and the door shut. Their footsteps went east along the stone and turned a corner and were gone.

Eli did not say anything else. He had already said the thing he came up here to say. Luca lay back against the slanted tiles. And above him the whole sky was strung, and still more constellations were appearing. The bright one at the corner had been the north star ten thousand years ago and would be again in about the same, and he found he could not remember which corner it was, and it did not seem to matter very much, and the gold continued arriving.

"Luca."

He had not heard him move. Eli was crouched over him and the whole sky was behind his head, and it took a moment to arrange because the gold was in the wrong places now, or he had turned without meaning to.

"Wake up."

"I'm awake."

"Then get up."

Eli took his wrist.

He did it the way a person does who has not considered whether it's allowed, and then he was going, and Luca went.

Along the ridge. The tiles went under his feet and one of them shifted and did not come loose. The glasses were still standing in the channel and one of them went over and Luca heard it break somewhere a long way down and neither of them stopped. There was a gap between the roof and the tower, an arm's width of nothing with the street at the bottom of it, and Eli crossed it in one step without breaking stride and Luca crossed it because his wrist was already on the other side.

Then the stairs.

They ran up the outside of the tower in a spiral of stairs, and the whole city was on his left going around and around. The rooftops. The market square with its awnings furled. The canal, then the barrier, then the rooftops again.

"Where are we going."

"Up."

Luca considered, somewhere at the back of it all, that he had not made any conscious decision about this. That there had been a moment on the roof where the question could have been asked and it had gone past, and that a sober man would have stopped by now and planted his feet and asked it.

The wind was stronger the higher they went. The gold threads was below him now instead of overhead. He could see the underside of the constellations at an angle that had not been available from the roof, and they went past in pieces, the harp, the square, the bright one at the corner, sliding by on his left with the city under them, and then the turn took them away.

He looked down once.

The roof they had been sitting on was very small and a long way below. Two red slopes of it, and the ridge running north, and the wet mark on the tiles where the glass had gone over.

The city came around again and it was further and there was less of it.

Eli's hand did not loosen.

The stairs ended at a door.

Old wood, black with age, iron across it in two bands, and no window anywhere on the last four turns of the climb.

Eli reached it and stopped and turned around, and Luca came up the last three steps and stopped as well, because his hand was still being held.

Eli was not breathing hard. Luca was, badly, and it took him a moment to understand that Eli was not out of breath at all and that whatever was happening in his face had nothing to do with the stairs.

"You said it wasn't going to happen," Eli said.

"I did."

"I want you to remember that you said that."

He pulled the door open, and the light came out of it.

It came flat across Luca's face at the height of a low sun and it was white and it was enormous, and his eyes shut on their own the way they do, and his arm was already going up too late, and the hand around his other one pulled forward.

He opened his eyes.

His first thought was that Eli had finished it. That the whole cloud had been strung from one edge to the other while his eyes were shut, every shape closed and hung, and he stood there a moment with the pleasure of it before he understood that nothing above him was gold. It was white. All of it was white, and it went far past any surface a hand could reach, layer behind layer behind layer, the near ones sharp and the far ones a kind of luminous dust, and the more he looked the more of them seem to have arrived.

His wrist was his own again. He did not remember being let go.

He looked down. Flowers were growing out of the seam between two blocks a hand's width from his face, small and blue and five-petaled, yellow at the center, the yellow very small against all that blue, and they had split the white stone.

He looked at them for a long moment. Then he walked forward, and the world fell away beneath him.

He was on a tower. White stone underfoot, with columns rising around him in the old manner with vines running up their fluting and out along the capitals, and past the edge the Garden fell in terraces, archway beneath archway, white walls smothered in climbing color, down and down until distance made a haze of it. Far below him a bird crossed from one terrace to another, and he watched it pass beneath his feet.

Flowers grew so thickly across that ground that the earth beneath them had been forgotten, white and scarlet and gold and violet and blue in more shades than the eye could keep separate, running in long drifts that followed the shape of the land the way current follows the floor of a river, pooling where the ground sank, thinning where it climbed. Water ran green and slow through the valley floor. White houses stood among the flowers with their doors open.

Then the wind came.

It started somewhere past seeing, out at the horizon where the ground rose to meet the sky and the flowers ran up into the stars, and from up here he could watch the whole of its arrival. It crossed the water and went up the far slope and the color of the entire hillside changed and changed back. It came through the houses and their open doors. It came up the terraces one after another, each level of climbing color turning over as it passed, nearer, nearer.

It reached him last of all, over his hands and through his hair and past him, and went on into the sky.

He tipped his head back.

He knew this place. From a lopsided lake poorly drawn on a napkin that he did not throw away. From five petals he had given to flowers he had never seen. From a grey ceiling of cloud hung with gold, a hand moving without hurry, forty-one shapes returned one by one to a sky that had never held them.

It had them all. Five, and the yellow at the center, and the crooked seven leaning east with its dim one at the bend, and a hundred thousand other things, numerous and luminous as the lights above.

One of them came loose.

It went out of its place in the shape and drew a white line down the whole height of the sky, so fast and so bright that it burned its own image onto the back of his eyes.

Another followed it. Then two more. Then the sky came apart in every quarter at once, stars leaving their positions in long slow arcs that crossed from edge to edge, each one trailing fire that thinned and hung and was still burning when the next came through it, so that the whole heaven was hatched over in bright silent lines, spending itself.

And the field took it. Every petal caught what the sky was losing and lit from within, mile after mile of it, the color rising up out of the ground until the Garden below him burned brighter than the heavens above, and the sky went on falling into it, pouring its ruin down without pause.

Nothing up there was where the Eli said it should be. Luca stood in the middle of it with his hands open at his sides and did not once look away.

Then a meteor came down the eastern quarter of the sky, and something interrupted it.

A silhouette, black against all that falling light. It had been there the whole time. The columns, the terraces, the burning field, and this, standing where the stone gave out onto open air, facing away.

The wind died.

The leaves stopped turning. On the terraces the flowers stood up straight and did not move again. Somewhere far below a stem righted itself and Luca heard it. A meteor crossed the entire sky in silence, and in the space it left he could hear his own heart, arriving late, once, twice, and the sound of his own breath going out, and nothing else at all. The columns cast no shadow. The silhouette of Eli at the edge did not move.

Luca hands opened at his sides.

Eli turned around.

His wings came open with him and the sound of it split the quiet like a sail taking wind, one enormous crack of air, and the wind of them reached Luca across the whole width of the tower and struck him in the face and went through his hair and through his shirt and the flowers on the terraces below flattened outward in a ring. White. Vast. The span of them swallowed the columns, swallowed the sky behind, and every falling star that had crossed the heavens that night came down into the feathers and stayed there, streak upon streak upon streak, until the wings burned with the whole gathered wreckage of the sky, brighter than the field, brighter than the tower, brighter than anything Luca had believed a body could carry.

Luca took a step back. And he could not breathe.

Eli’s arms were open. His hands turned out. His throat, his chest, every place a blade goes, offered forward without a thought, and the light poured over his upturned face, and his eyes were already on Luca and had been for longer than the turn.

He was young. Standing in all that fire with his arms open, he was so young.

"Look out that way." His arm swept wide, out past the columns. "Past the water. Do you see the edge? Way out there, where the flowers thin out and the ground just stops."

Luca looked. Far off, at the limit of everything, a line where the color gave up.

"On the other side of that line there are people who wake up hungry. And people who bury each other."

A star came down behind him and lit the whole of his face and passed.

"That is where I am going next."

He turned his head at last, following his own arm out toward the grey, and whatever was out there, he was looking straight at it.

"I'll go down through the valley first. Over the water and up the other side. And when I get to the top of that hill the flowers will run down the far side, and I'll keep going. Out over the water. To the places where people are. To the streets they are sleeping in tonight, through the ruins they have already given up on."

He came a step forward.

"Nobody will have to come here. Nobody will have to find it." Another step. "It is going to reach them. Every one of them, wherever they are."

He was close now. Close enough that Luca could see the falling sky reflected in his eyes, the wind flowing through the white feathers,

"That is what we are going to do."

His hand rose between them, palm open, and stopped there. Nothing was in it.

"We go over that edge and we do not stop. We do not stop until that's the world." His eyes were bright and they were fixed on Luca and they had not left him. "Until that's the whole world, for as long as I am alive, and that is so much time, Luca."

The sky came down around him in white lines, burning, and he stood in the middle of it.

"We have so much time."

He was still looking at Luca. He had never once stopped looking at Luca. That expression that had no business being on a human face, held there, open, waiting to be taken or refused, and Luca would have given anything for him to turn around.
