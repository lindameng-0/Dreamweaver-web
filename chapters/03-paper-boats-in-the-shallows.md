title: Paper Boats in the Shallows
vigil: VIGIL I — THE COMMISSION
folio: 41

There are doors into the Shallows all over the city, if you know to stop mistaking them for other things. A stairwell that goes down one step more than it did yesterday. A canal at slack tide holding the reflection of a moon the sky does not currently own. Riku used the door he trusted: the gap between the third and fourth chime of the Kanzaki bell, which is one second wide and always unlocked for licence-holders.

The Shallows received him the way they receive everyone — mid-stride, ankle-deep, in the first minute of the whole city's sleep at once. As far as sight went in every direction, dark water lay smooth as lacquer, printed with the drifting lights of other minds arriving. And on the water, boats: thousands of small paper boats, each folded from a page of something, each carrying its little cargo out from the waking world. The things people meant to say and never did, ferried nightly to no address.

The ferryman was poling toward him before Riku had wrung out his cuffs, and the sight was the same small shock it always was: the white wings, ragged as a coat worn to church and war both, folded against a back held very straight; the blue scarf going its own way in a wind nobody else was issued. Haku Shirasagi, last of his line, ferryman of the unsaid, owner of the only honest smile Riku knew.

“You only visit when you're breaking regulations,” Haku said, by way of good evening. He looked at Riku a moment longer, and the smile organised itself into something gentler. “Ah. You're going under the weave. Get in, then. Mind the cargo.”

***

They poled out across the first minute of sleep, between the paper flotillas. Riku read hulls despite himself; everyone does. I forgive you, one said, folded sharp and never sent. You were right about me, said another. A very small one, barely seaworthy, said only stay. Haku steered around that one with particular care, the way you would step around a sleeping child.

The fare for passage below is fixed and ancient and cannot be paid in money. At the marker post, where the water forgets to stay shallow, Haku shipped the pole and held out one hand, and Riku paid: a secret, spoken aloud for the first time, which is the only kind that spends. He said it quietly. It was about the lacquered box, and the number roughly no longer meant. One feather on Haku's left wing went grey as ash, and the ferryman tucked the secret away somewhere behind his scarf without hearing it, which is the whole mercy of his profession.

“Eleven nights, for a woman the Loom can't see,” Haku said, taking up the pole again. “You know what's under the weave, Riku. You know who watches down there.” The boat slid past the marker. The water beneath them stopped pretending to have a bottom. “I'll wait at the post,” he said, “as long as the wings hold. If you hear applause — walk out mid-performance. Promise me. It's the only exit they honour.”
