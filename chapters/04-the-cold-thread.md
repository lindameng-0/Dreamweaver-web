title: The Cold Thread
vigil: VIGIL I — THE COMMISSION
folio: 63

Below the Shallows the world is fabric. There is no politer way to put it. The dreams of the city run in strands past the falling body — warm where they are joyful, cool where they grieve, knotted where the dreamer has told themselves a story so often it has set. A licensed weaver falls slowly, hands out, reading the weave the way a blind man reads a beloved book. Riku had made this descent a thousand times, and it had never once felt like falling until tonight.

He was looking for absence. It is the hardest search there is. Warmth calls to the palm from a strand away, but a thread that carries nothing announces itself only by touch, and only if you are foolish enough to be moving slowly, and only if you have never learned the sensible fear of cold things in the dark.

He found it at the depth where the light from the Shallows gives out — a single strand running against the grain of the whole cloth, crosswise, wrong. It did not glow. It did not hum. It was cold the way a doorknob is cold in an empty house, cold with recent absence, and it ran taut and deliberate into the black below the last charted layer of the weave, toward the place the licence in his head said in small firm letters that no needle lawfully goes.

***

Every weaver knots their thread in a signature; you learn your dead teachers by their knots the way you learn handwriting. Riku brought the cold strand to his eyes to read the knot, and the dark under the city held very still while it let him understand what he was touching.

It was a whip-finish knot, tied left-handed, with the second loop doubled — a flourish invented by an eleven-year-old to annoy an older brother who tied everything by the book. Nobody else had ever used it. Nobody else had ever been allowed to learn it, on the inventor's strict instruction, delivered around a grin missing one tooth. The knot was Nagi's. The thread was Nagi's. The thread was cold, and it was carrying that cold up from below, steadily, like a wick drawing in reverse.

Nagi Amano had walked past the Selvage eight years ago, following a dream the way other children follow a cat, and the Loom Office had struck their name from the registries with the gentleness of institutions, and Riku had mended four hundred strangers' nightmares since because it was the only grief he could reach. The Loom does not carry the threads of the lost. It cannot. And yet.

Above him, eleven nights of contract. Below him, a knot no dead child could have tied, going down where the weave gives way to the dark that watches back. Riku hung between them for the space of four heartbeats, which is a long time mid-fall, and then he did what he had signed no paper promising not to do. He took the cold thread in his fist, and he followed it down.
