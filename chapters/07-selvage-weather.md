title: Selvage Weather
vigil: VIGIL II — THE DESCENT
folio: 140

Sayo was waiting for him at the marker post in the Shallows, which was four impossibilities in one place, and she was standing on the water as if it were a floor, because — as she pointed out, before he could spend breath on amazement — a surface that exists only for dreamers can hardly object to being walked on by a woman who does not dream.

“You sat down,” she said. It was not an accusation. It was bookkeeping. “Eleven years I have sat in that theatre alone, and on the sixth night of my contract, my weaver walks in, watches my grief performed by someone who loves it, and sits down beside me.” She looked at him with the level dark eyes that the Loom could not find. “I felt it, you know. The seat beside mine going warm. First change in the programme in eleven years. I am not going to pretend I want a refund.”

Haku poled them out past the last flotillas, to where the Shallows stop being shallow and start being edge. The Selvage announces itself as weather long before it arrives as geography: a wind with unravelling in it, carrying loose ends of dreams that were begun and abandoned — a staircase without a house, a birthday without a date, thin bright threads of almost-was streaming past like the beginning of snow. The ferryman's wings were greyer by three secrets than they had been a week ago, and he flew the boat now more than he steered it.

***

At the frayed edge of everything, where the great cloth of the city's sleep gives way to its own hem, the cold thread rose out of the deep and ran on, out, past the last intact strand, into the streaming dark. And out there — at the very end of reach, at the distance the eye insists on calling near because the alternative is unbearable — a figure stood in the unravelling wind, holding the far end.

Eight years had not made Nagi older. The Selvage does not spend its residents; it keeps them, the way a page keeps a pressed flower. Small, precise, chin lifted, left hand working an absent knot in the streaming threads — second loop doubled — and eyes fixed on Riku with an expression he knew from four hundred strangers' nightmares and had never once been able to mend: the expression of someone who has been rehearsing a line so long they no longer believe in the play.

Nagi's mouth moved. The wind took the words and unravelled them like everything else, but Riku had spent a childhood reading that mouth across noisy rooms, and the line was the line from the Theatre, given back: you were supposed to follow me. And then, because the Amano children never could leave a flourish alone, one more word, mouthed slowly, with the ghost of a grin missing one tooth: so?

Riku stood up in the boat. Behind him, Sayo said his name — level, unhurried, a tether announcing itself. Beneath him, the last of his own dreams shifted in the lacquered box, and he finally counted them, and the count was one. Ahead, his sibling held out the cold thread across the unravelling dark, an invitation eight years overdue. The wind rose. The hem of the world hummed like a struck string. Here ends the Second Vigil.
