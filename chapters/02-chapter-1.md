"Don't be afraid."

Eli's hand tightened around the blade. The figure before him stumbled back, breathing fast, but Eli's eyes held steady. Patient. Almost kind.

The strike was quick. He felt the give of flesh, the terrible ease of it. There should have been a scream. A collapse. The things that always followed.

Nothing.

No blood came. The wound opened and from inside it, light. Then petals. Dozens of them, amber and heavy, spilling out where blood should have pooled. Sunflowers pushed through the gap, their heads too large for their stems, bowing under the weight of all that gold.

Between one breath and the next the world changed. Eli stood waist-deep in a field that stretched past the edges of seeing. Sunflowers, thousands of them, all turned toward a sun that was either rising or dying. He could never tell which. The light had no name he knew. It fell on everything and made it the same color, the color of the hundredth warm day.

Eli let go of the blade. It was gone before it touched the soil.

He reached for the nearest bloom and cupped it in his hands. The petals were warm. They were always warm. He held it the way you might hold a small bird, loosely, so it could leave if it wanted to, though it never did.

"Perfect for the garden," he said, though there was no one left to hear. He didn't mind. He never tired of this. However many times he came here, however many dreams he walked through to arrive, the field was always the same and always worth it.

He closed his eyes. Let the rest of it come. The smell of earth after rain. The warmth of sun on the back of his neck. The particular sweetness that lived somewhere between honey and something older than honey, something he could taste at the roof of his mouth if he breathed in deeply enough. He breathed in. He breathed in again. The scent grew until it seemed to come from inside his own chest, as though the field of sunflowers was finally his own.

***

Eli opened his eyes and the petals were gone. The scent stayed a moment longer, fading the way warmth fades from a cup you've been holding, and then that was gone too.

He pushed the drapes aside as he crossed the room. Layer after layer of thin fabric, each one catching the light differently, until the last curtain parted and the morning came through. The blue was wrong. A few shades too dim, the way it always was, and he blinked at it twice before he stopped trying.

The terracotta roofs had caught the early light and were holding it, amber and warm, as if some deity's hand had polished every stone and tile in the night. Smoke climbed from chimneys in loose spirals that frayed apart before they got anywhere. Flowering vines spilled from window boxes along the residential quarter, their blooms opening with the sun the way they did every morning. The air coming through the gap in the window tasted clean, tinged with the taste of bread and lavender.

And at the center of it all, the Skyscraper.

It caught the sun and broke it apart and threw the pieces where they weren't wanted, across rooftops and into windows and down streets that had been perfectly fine in shade. Glass and steel, taller than anything else by a factor that felt personal. Necessary, Maeve had said. Progress, Maeve had called it.

He could fly straight there. Cut across the northern sector, be early for once. But the route would take him along the barrier, and his gaze found the edge of it now, that thin purple shimmer holding back the wasteland that went on and on to the horizon, and he looked away. Better not.

His wings opened in one motion, broad and catching the light, and he stepped off the windowsill into the morning.

Four years of this flight and he still found things. A window box on the second story of a building he passed every day, freshly planted with morning glories that hadn't been there yesterday. A fountain in the eastern square whose spray threw up small rainbows that lasted two, sometimes three seconds before they broke apart. His shadow slid across rooftops and down into streets where merchants were throwing open their shutters, and the sound of it came up to him in cheerful bangs and voices calling to one another, and Eli let himself climb a little higher just to hear it all at once.

The market square opened below him in color. Awnings unfurling. Red for the bakeries, blue for the cloth merchants, green for the florists. He had watched this happen so many times that he knew the order, knew which merchant was always first and which was always last and which one's awning stuck on the left side and needed a good shove. He knew all of it. He tucked his wings and dropped.

The landing was soft. The cobblestones were warm under his feet. The crowd parted around him without breaking stride, the way water parts around a stone it has learned to expect.

"Morning, Tomas." He clasped the fisherman's hand without breaking stride. "Shoulder feeling better?" Tomas said something about the cold, and Eli was already past him. "Leah." A nod to the silver-haired woman. "Northern patrol quiet?" She smiled her yes. "And Darian! Your mentor mentioned the roof. You handled the southern corner yourself?" The boy flushed so deeply Eli could see it from three paces away.

He moved through them the way he always moved through them, a hand here, a word there, just enough to show he had seen them. And he had. He always had. He knew their names and their friends’ names and the small things that worried them at night. It was easy to know people when the city was this size. When had a stranger last walked through this market? When had Eli last learned a name he didn't already know? He couldn't remember.

"General!"

A voice he knew. He turned and there was Mina, cutting between stalls with flour on her apron and her brown hair going curly at the edges where the oven heat had caught it.

"The beloved general returns to the common folk." She was grinning. "I was beginning to think you'd vanished entirely."

"Vanished? Me?" Eli pressed a hand to his chest. "I've been incredibly visible. Ask anyone. Well, don't ask Maeve. Or the other generals. Or anyone who's tried to schedule a meeting with me this week." He took the loaf she held out. One edge was slightly burnt. He smiled anyway. "Your temperature control's getting so much better."

Mina flexed her fingers. A flicker of orange danced between them, there and gone with a flick of her wrist. "Practice makes perfect. Isn't that what you said to me?"

"I also said to let me pay you this time." He reached for the pouch at his belt. She swatted his hand away.

"Absolutely not. Consider it payment for the nightmares." Her face brightened. "I haven't had a single bad dream since I visited the Garden, Eli. Not one."

He broke off a corner of the bread. Cinnamon first, then apple, then a sourness underneath both that wasn't quite right. He chewed and swallowed. "I'm glad it helped."

"It did more than help." She touched the flower in her hair, her fingers resting on the petals the way you might touch something you were afraid of losing. "I really feel different now."

"Since when did the bakery branch out into floristry?" Eli asked, nodding at the bloom.

"I couldn't help myself after seeing them there." She looked down at her hands. "I've been growing a few from seeds I found after the storm. They're never quite as beautiful as yours, though. No matter what I do they're just missing something." She looked up. "How is it that the ones you grow are so perfect?"

Eli smiled. He did not answer.

"Well, you have to tell me one day." She pulled another bloom from her basket, freshly cut, and held it out. "For you."

"Thank you." He took it carefully. The color was several shades duller than it should have been, a white that was almost grey, a white that was trying. He tucked it behind his ear with enough formality to make her laugh, and the laugh was worth more than the flower.

"I'd rather be there than here," Mina said. Her gaze drifted toward the Skyscraper. "General Maeve says I have another three months of guard duty before I can go back. But every day feels like a year when I know what's waiting."

Eli broke off another piece of bread. The Skyscraper caught the sun from here too, its glass throwing the light back in hard geometric lines that cut across the old stone buildings around it like something from a different city. Which, in a way, it was.

"I understand," he said. "I'll talk to her."

She beamed. "Thank you, General."

"Just Eli. Please." He gestured at himself with the remaining crust. "Do I look like a general to you?"

"You look like someone who's about to be late for a meeting with General Maeve." Mina's smirk told him she'd been waiting to say this. "Don't let her catch you with that flower. She'll assume you've been slacking off again."

"You have excellent timing." He was already lifting off. "Oh. Before I forget. The baker's son has been asking about you. Subtly, he thinks."

"Define subtly."

"'Has Mina been by lately?' Every single morning. Sometimes twice."

Mina groaned. "I was afraid of that."

"Want me to have a word? I can be diplomatic. Or Seth can be less diplomatic."

"I'll handle it. But thanks." She was already backing toward her bakery, waving. "Go! Before Maeve sends a search party."

"She'd have to catch me first," Eli called back.

Eli saluted her with the loaf, and flew toward that gleaming eyesore of a skyscraper which somehow represented both their greatest achievement and their most visible compromise. Maeve's pride and joy, a beacon of modernity that had consumed resources they could scarcely afford. The glare from its windows made him narrow his eyes slightly. Eli knew that Maeve would be waiting, her schedule meticulously planned to the minute. The white flower tucked behind his ear would annoy her far more than his near tardiness, and the thought made Eli smile as he soared toward the heart of Auralis, the taste of cinnamon and apples still sweet and slightly sour on his tongue.

Maeve was waiting in the courtyard. She stood in the Skyscraper's shadow with her tablet held at her side the way someone might hold a clutch at a dinner party, and she was looking at her watch. She looked up when Eli landed, looked at his face, looked the white flower behind his ear, and stayed there for a moment longer than it needed to.

"You're on time," she said. "How nice."

"I live to exceed your extraordinarily low expectations." Eli offered her the bread. She glanced at it the way one might glance at a child's drawing held up for approval.

"Thank you, no." She turned and led the way through the glass doors without checking whether he followed. Her heels were sharp on the lobby floor. "You know, your quarters on the eastern wing still get the most beautiful morning light. It seems such a waste to let it sit empty."

"That's very kind. But the sunrise looks different from every rooftop. I'd hate to see it from only one."

"You're difficult to find, Eli." She pressed the elevator call button. Her nails were clean and filed short, practical in a way that still managed to look deliberate. "People worry."

"Yet here I am." The doors closed. "People know where to find me when they need me. Besides, I do have a garden to tend to."

"Resources are limited, as always," Maeve said. She was watching the floor numbers climb, her voice carrying the even tone of someone reading a weather report. "Some of the military sectors have grown restless. And because of this, General Rae has naturally become restless as well." She paused, then added, as though it were an afterthought, "Most of the generals are beginning to reconsider the Garden's priority."

"Why?" Eli asked, turning to face her. "Wasn't Auralis's ideal always to create a paradise away from suffering? Why is that suddenly unimportant? Mina asked me about returning to the Garden. If people are so eager to go back, why not let them?"

Maeve's gaze stayed on the ascending numbers. "Mina," she said, as though filing the name somewhere she might or might not retrieve it later.

"She's a baker. She—"

"I'm sure she's lovely." Maeve said. "The Garden is essential. I've always believed that. It gives people something to look forward to, and that alone justifies the investment." A pause. She smoothed the same crease again. "Though I do think we could discuss the timeline. There's no rush, is there? Good things take time."

The elevator slowed as it approached the upper floors. Eli moved to the glass wall, looking down at the view that always made something twist in his chest. Auralis spread below them like a perfect circle inscribed within the purple barrier, but most of his vision was filled with what lay beyond. Perhaps this was why he avoided living in the Skyscraper. Here, he couldn't pretend the border didn't exist. If Auralis were just a bit larger, just expansive enough to hide that disquieting boundary.

"How many more will suffer while we wait?" Eli asked quietly. He watched a merchant's awning unfurl far below, a square of red no larger than a fingernail from this height. "How many more will lose hope?"

Maeve was quiet for a moment. She adjusted the cuff of her sleeve, a motion so small and precise it might have been involuntary.

"Hope," she repeated, turning the word over to examine its underside. "You have such a talent for that word, Eli. I've always thought so." Maeve's expression became shuttered. "We manage what we have. We keep people safe." She turned to look at him. "Isn't that what you fought for, General Eli? To keep them safe?"

Eli touched the flower behind his ear. Its petals had already begun to wilt. "I fought for more than just survival," he said. "I fought for life."

Maeve looked at him. For a moment something moved behind her eyes, quick and unreadable, the way a curtain shifts when a window is opened in another room. Then it settled.

"That's a beautiful way to put it," she said. Her voice was warm. "I do hope you get the chance to see it through."

The elevator chimed. The doors opened.

Around the circular table, a dozen generals were on their feet. Voices layered over voices. Someone's fist came down on polished wood and the tactical pins jumped in their slots. The morning light through the floor-to-ceiling windows caught the metal and threw it back in small bright flashes that nobody noticed.

Eli moved forward without hesitation. His wings spread just enough to make the space around him larger, and he stepped between two generals who had been leaning toward each other in a way that suggested the next thing to happen would not be verbal.

"Everyone," he said, his voice cutting through calmly. "Perhaps we could all take a breath. Whatever's happening, we'll solve it better with clear heads."

Sylvia whirled toward him. Her uniform carried more combat ribbons than any other general's, and she wore them the way some people wear scars, with a kind of furious pride.

"Of course you bring him of all people," she said to Maeve. Her gaze dropped to the bloom behind Eli's ear. "Did you bring flowers for everyone, or just yourself?"

"Good to see you too, Sylvia." Eli's smile didn't falter. He removed the flower and placed it on the table with a gentleness that had no business being in this room. "What happened?"

“Citadel sent a negotiator," Rae explained, her voice carefully neutral. She stood at the head of the table, hands clasped behind her back, "Commander Jax requested communication."

"And you denied it," Eli concluded, taking his seat.

"Of course we denied it," Sylvia didn’t sit. "We let that woman transmit a message instead. The same garbage they always feed us. Surrender your resources, surrender your people, surrender your pathetic dream. They've been saying it for four years." Each phrase dripped with contempt. "I ended the call before she could finish her script."

"You terminated official communication without authorization." Maeve did not raise her voice.

"We authorized it," Rae gestured to the other generals. "The vote was seven to three."

"What exactly did she say before the transmission ended?" Eli asked.

"Nothing we haven't heard before," Sylvia snapped. "We've been passive for too long. The military sector is overflowing with recruits we can't properly equip. People are tired of being contained."

"Yes," Maeve said.

Sylvia stopped. Yes was not an argument.

"You're quite right," Maeve continued. She folded her hands in her lap, one over the other. "We should discuss the numbers. Rae, how many units can we reasonably commit without compromising the northern watch?"

"Don't." Sylvia cut Rae off before she could open her mouth. "Don't do that. I'm not talking about numbers, Maeve. I'm talking about people suffocating inside this fishbowl while you sit here with your tablet and your schedules and your patience." She spat the last word. "How long have we been waiting for the right moment? Another year? Another decade? Tell me, how long have we been waiting, because I have lost count."

"I understand your frustration, Sylvia. I do." Maeve turned back to her tablet. "But right now, our people are safe. Would you squander it on pride?”

"It's not pride," General Emerson's voice came from the far end of the table, surprising enough that several heads turned. He rarely spoke. "It's survival. Every day we wait, they grow stronger. We have weapons now, soldiers. The Military Sector stands ready."

Sylvia's hand was still on the table. She looked from Maeve to Emerson to Rae, and her mouth opened and closed once.

Eli cleared his throat.

The room settled the way rooms settled when he did this, in layers, voices dropping, chairs turning, faces finding his.

"I've been thinking," he said, "about what we promised each other."

He let that sit. Promises were a specific kind of weight in Auralis. Everyone in this room had made them.

"When we built this city, we said it would be the beginning. That everything after would follow. I believed that. I still believe it. But I have been standing in this room for four years, and every time I come here the conversation is smaller. We used to talk about what we would build. Now we talk about what we can afford to keep."

"Pretty words from someone who's never stood on the front lines," Sylvia said. But she said it quietly.

Eli smiled. "Have any of you been to the Garden recently?"

A few glances exchanged. Maeve's thumbnail found the edge of her tablet case.

"You should. You really should. Because when you stand in the Garden, you understand something that is very difficult to understand from inside this room. You understand that the world is supposed to look like that. The Garden is what everything was always meant to be. Every field, every city, every piece of earth the Metropolis has turned into wasteland or market or barracks." His voice was warm. Almost conversational. "And I find it strange, I find it genuinely strange, that we have built the most beautiful thing in the world and our highest ambition is to keep it."

He moved to the window. The barrier shimmered in the middle distance. Beyond it, that familiar grey, flat and endless, and somewhere past the horizon, the Metropolis, which no one in this room had ever seen and everyone in this room had spent their lives beneath.

"We have been having the same argument in this room for four years. The terms change, the words change, but the shape is always the same. Can we survive them. Can we outlast them. And sometimes, when we are feeling particularly desperate, can we find some way to exist alongside them." He paused. "I have listened to this argument every single time, and every single time I have wanted to ask a question that I think we are all afraid to hear."

He turned back to the room. His eyes moved from face to face.

"Why are we trying to survive them?"

Silence.

"The Metropolis was built. Someone decided it would exist, and it did. Someone decided it would rule, and it does. And we have spent so long living underneath it that we have started to believe that a world with the Metropolis at the top is the only world available to us. And so we argue about survival, about whether to hold the barrier or extend it, and every version of that argument ends the same way, because every version of that argument accepts that they are above us."

His voice had not changed. It was still warm, still unhurried, still the voice of someone describing a garden he loved.

"I would like to see it fall."

The word landed in the room the way a stone lands in still water. No one had said it in here before. Not once. Not in four years.

"I know we do not say this. But I keep thinking about the Garden. I would like someone in this room to explain to me why it is madness to believe that a city which grew from the scorched soil, which raised a barrier against the end of the world, which built the Garden, cannot do this too." He turned back. His eyes were bright. His expression was entirely serious, and for a moment he looked very young, and then the moment passed and he still looked very young. "Maybe the Metropolis is everything they say it is and we are everything they say we are. Maybe we walk toward it and we will be destroyed."

He said it gently. And if anyone in the room heard the edge underneath, they did not mention it.

"I would like to find out," he said. "I think we owe ourselves that much."

The silence after was different from the silences before. Eli could see it in the way Sylvia's hand unclenched on the table. In how Emerson straightened. Rae's fingers found the gold ring on her index finger and turned it once, twice. Her eyes moved between faces, counting something. When she spoke, her voice had Sylvia's urgency in it, though her hands did not.

"Perhaps it really is time we took action. The residential sectors grow restless. They see the barrier as a prison now. Besides, Seth reports—"

"Seth reports what serves his agenda," Maeve said mildly. Then she looked at Eli.

He was still by the window. The light behind him caught the edges of his wings, and he did not seem to know this.

"Anyhow, that was lovely.” Maeve said, “ But I do wonder how the Garden will manage while you’re away.”

Eli hesitated. His gaze flicked briefly to the window, toward the direction of the out-of-sight Garden. But he was sure it was there, somewhere.

Before he could respond, the doors burst open. A young scout stumbled in, uniform disheveled. "Generals. Citadel has fired the first shot. Seth's forces have engaged at the eastern perimeter."

The room fell silent.

"Engaged? Without authorization?" Maeve looked up.

"Seth said..." the scout swallowed hard, "Citadel force was attempting to breach the barrier. They're requesting reinforcements. Heavy resistance."

Eli stood perfectly still, the flower he held in his hand suddenly seeming incongruously bright against his pale face.

"Well," Sylvia said, a fierce grin spreading across her features, "seems the decision's been made for us." She was already in the process of reaching for her jacket. "I'll coordinate the eastern flank myself. My troops are battle-ready."

"Yes," Maeve said. "You're quite right. We should discuss the deployment numbers. Rae, how many units can we reasonably commit without compromising the northern watch?"

She looked at Sylvia, then at Maeve, then at the tactical map. "If we deploy a full unit east, we need our combat-experienced generals maintaining command structure here. The northern and western perimeters can't hold without senior leadership on rotation."

Sylvia's jaw tightened. "So we send someone who can operate alone.”

The room was still arguing when Maeve looked up from her tablet. She waited. Not impatiently.

When the voices dropped low enough she said, "Eli."

He turned. She smiled. It was a good smile, the kind that made people feel honored.

"Would you mind terribly?"

Four words. The smile stayed exactly where it was.

"I was planning to expand the Garden," Eli said, his voice perfectly level. He adjusted his wings minutely.

"Take whoever you like. But not too many." Maeve said. "And rest assured, the Garden will still be there when you return.”

Eli smiled, the expression reaching his eyes with practiced ease. "Of course it will." He plucked the flower from behind his ear and placed it on the table, the gesture deliberate, unhurried.

“Good luck,” Sylvia made a sound that might have been a grudging approval or otherwise a mocking laugh as she stood and turned to leave. “I heard you haven’t been outside in ages.”

Maeve touched his arm as the others filed out. Her fingers rested there for a moment, light and brief.

"Be careful," she said. Her voice was low enough that only he could hear. "I would hate to lose you."

She held his gaze. Then she let go, and smoothed the front of her coat, and walked away.

No one noticed how his fingers, when they briefly brushed against the edge of the table, pressed just a fraction too hard against the polished surface. Or how, when the elevator doors closed behind him, his reflection in the glass showed eyes focused not on the battle ahead, but on the distant patch of green visible through the windows. The Garden, peaceful under the morning sun, suddenly seemed very far away.
