title: The Erased Folio
vigil: VIGIL II — THE DESCENT
folio: 117

He woke on the floor of the Somnium Records, which should have been impossible in at least four ways, with the taste of theatre dust in his mouth and a librarian's lamp shining directly into his conscience.

“Seventy years,” said Professor Ibara, from behind the lamp, “I have kept this archive, and in seventy years, do you know how many people have exited the Theatre by sitting down? You are the second.” She let that arithmetic sit exactly long enough. “The first shelves herself under O.”

The Records go down as far as the city goes up. Every dream surrendered since the founding is here, bound in folios by year and street and sleeper, and the aisles between the stacks have the weather of the dreams they keep — a breath of sea-fog off the drowning years, dry heat down the aisle of the great fever, and everywhere the paper-and-lightning smell of a hundred million nights filed in order. Ibara walked him through it with the lamp held high, past his own aisle, which he did not look at, to a lectern at the heart of the stacks where one folio lay open under glass.

Folio 91. He knew the number; every apprentice copies the famous line out of it. Every city is two cities. You are a citizen of the one you forget by morning. What the apprentice copies do not show is the rest of the page, and the rest of the page was gone — not torn, not inked out, but erased in the old full sense, the fibres themselves persuaded they had always been blank. Only the quotation survived, floating in white, like the last window lit in a demolished street.

***

“The page recorded the first weaver,” Ibara said. “The licence before all licences. Who took it, what it cost, and what was promised to the dark under the weave in exchange for permission to stitch above it.” She lifted the glass and turned the folio over, and her voice did a thing he had never heard a voice do in the Records: it dropped to a whisper out of respect for something that was not the silence. “I have spent every night since erasing my erasure. It grows back, you see. The blank grows back. Someone down there holds the original, and keeps renewing the copy, and I have been losing a war of handwriting with the Theatre for seventy years.”

“Why show me?” said Riku, who was reading the blank the way weavers read thread, and finding one thing in it: a knot of absence, left-handed, second loop doubled, pressed into the page like a signature under paint.

“Because the first weaver's contract comes due,” said Ibara, “when the last weaver stops paying. There is one licence left on my registry, Mr. Amano. Yours. And you have begun, this very week, to spend dreams you do not have — which means the dark is extending you credit.” She closed the folio. “Nothing under the weave extends credit, boy. It buys. The question you must take back up with you is: what, exactly, does it think it is buying?”
