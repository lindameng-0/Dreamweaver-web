title: The Unslept Tailor
vigil: VIGIL I — THE COMMISSION
folio: 1

The first rule of weaving is that you do not look at the dreamer's face. Riku broke it the way he broke all rules — slowly, politely, with his hands already raised to frame the dark. Between his fingers the nightmare opened like a stage: a figure reaching upward, an audience of shadows, applause as soft as snow settling on a roof. Someone else's grief, he reminded himself, and began to unpick the seam of it.

His workshop sat above a laundry on Kanzaki Street, and it smelled of steam and cedar and the particular sweetness of thread that has been through other people's sleep. By day it looked like any tailor's room: a long table, a wall of spools, a licence in a black frame with the wax seal of the Loom Office pressed into its corner. By night the spools glowed faintly, each one wound with an hour of somebody's dreaming, labelled in his careful hand. Grief, cold-fast, do not wash. Joy, colourfast, fades anyway.

He had been the youngest weaver ever licensed, and he was now, at twenty-six, very nearly the last. The others had retired the way weavers retire: quietly, mid-sentence, asleep. It is an occupational certainty. A weaver mends the dreams of strangers by paying out his own, one for one, the way a lamp spends oil. Riku kept his remaining dreams in a lacquered box in his head and did not count them, because counting them was the beginning of the end. He knew roughly. Roughly was survivable.

***

The summons came at the hour when the laundry's last kettle went cold, carried by a paper crane with a wingbeat like a page being turned. He knew the fold of it before he broke the seal. Madame Orihara did not send cranes for small things. She had not sent one at all in three years, which had suited them both.

The message was four lines long, which for her was an aria. A commission. A client of consequence. A nightmare, recurring, of unusual construction. And then the line he read five times, standing in the cold with the crane dissolving into blank paper in his palm: The client, you will find, does not dream. Come anyway.

Every child of the city knows the arithmetic of sleep. You lie down, you surrender the night's dreaming to the Loom, and the Loom weaves it with everyone else's into the great cloth that keeps the city's other half lit. You cannot opt out. You cannot be missed. The Loom reaches every pillow in the province the way rain reaches every roof. A person who does not dream is not rare — a person who does not dream is not possible.

Riku put on his good coat, the one with thread from his own first mended nightmare sewn into the hem for luck he did not believe in. He looked once at the licence on the wall, once at the box in his head. Then he went out into the dark above the laundry steam, and the city, which had been pretending to be asleep, opened one bright eye to watch him go.
