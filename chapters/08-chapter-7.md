There was a room. The ceiling was plaster and old and someone had repainted it recently enough that the edges where the new coat met the old still showed. Cracks ran from the corner above the window toward the center. Fourteen, if you counted the seam where the plaster had dried unevenly. The window faced east. He knew this because the light that came through it in the early hours was thin and grey, and by midmorning it had crossed the floor to the foot of the bed and turned warm.

He did not remember being brought here. He remembered the sky and the dirt under his palms and the sound the building made as it went on falling. He remembered Maeve's shoes on uneven ground, a pace that did not change when it reached him. The pull upward. The thing his ribs did in response. And then a period that was either very long or very short during which the ground moved beneath him and he was not the one moving it.

He did not remember the barrier, or the gates, or the stairs to wherever this room was.

Maeve had not asked him anything. He was certain of this because it was the only thing from that night he could place with confidence. The walk back, however long it was, had been silent.

The room had a chair. Wood, with a straight back, pushed against the wall beneath the window. A small table beside the bed held a ceramic cup with water in it that had been there long enough for a fine layer of dust to settle on the surface. A shelf on the far wall held three books and a folded cloth and nothing else. He did not know whose room this was. It was not his. His room in Auralis was on Eli's floor, and this was not that.

The door had been closed when he woke. It stayed closed. Nobody knocked.

The first day was the light. It moved across the floor in its slow arc and he watched it the way you watch water fill a basin, without interest but with the impossibility of looking away. He was aware of his body as a collection of things that hurt. His ribs on the left side had been healed by hands he was unaware of. He could still feel the tissue around it still flinched from deep breaths. The shoulder where the first piece of ceiling had driven him into the console. Something in his lower back that shifted when he breathed and sent a thin bright line through him each time it shifted. He catalogued these carefully.

In the late morning someone came. A woman he did not know. Older, grey at the temples, wearing the plain clothes of the residential quarter. She carried a wooden case that she set on the chair without speaking. She opened it. Bottles. Linen. A small glass jar with a salve that smelled of camphor and something green beneath it.

She looked at his ribs from the foot of the bed. She looked at the temple where dried blood had matted into his hair. She stepped forward. She reached for his face.

His hand closed around her wrist.

The rest of him had not moved. He was on his back, his breathing the same shallow rhythm, his eyes on the ceiling. Only the hand. It had come off the sheets faster than anything in this room should have moved and it held her wrist with the fingers closed and the grip settled and his body so still it could have been a different person entirely. His eyes had not left the ceiling.The hand did what it did and the man it was attached to lay there and breathed and looked at the cracks in the plaster as though nothing below his shoulder concerned him.

She did not pull. She held still. She looked at the hand on her wrist and then at his face, and his face gave her nothing.

His fingers opened. One by one, in the order they had closed, as though the hand were putting itself away. His arm returned to the sheet.

She stepped back. She did not dare step forward again. She set the linen and the jar on the table beside the cup of water and she placed the roll of bandage beside them and she looked at the arrangement once and adjusted the jar a centimeter to the left.

"Every four hours," she said.

She closed the case. She left. The door closed behind her with a sound that was softer than it needed to be.

The jar sat on the table. The linen sat beside it, still rolled, untouched. He did not apply the salve every four hours. He applied it once, in the late afternoon, when the pressure below the line had climbed to a register that made it difficult to think about anything else. The bandage he did not touch at all. After that the jar and the linen and the roll of bandage sat on the table beside the water and all of them gathered their separate dusts.

Later, someone came to the door. Two knocks. A pause. A tray appeared on the floor outside some time after the footsteps left. Bread, cheese, an apple. He brought the tray inside and set it on the desk beside the water. He ate the apple. He left the rest.

He lay on the bed. The cracks in the ceiling were the same. He counted them again, though the number had not changed and would not change, and the act of counting was no longer verification. It was the only motion left in him that did not require him to move.

Fourteen.

On the fourth day he stood at the window for the first time.

The glass was warm from the afternoon. Auralis opened below him, the terracotta and stone, the vines along the residential quarter, the colored awnings of the market. And past the rooftops, past the edge of the city, the barrier. He found it without looking for it. A thin purple line at the perimeter, the same color from this side. Beyond it the grey, flat and endless, going on and on to where the horizon swallowed it.

His hand on the glass. The warmth going through his palm and stopping there.

He tried to look east. The window faced south and the angle was wrong, but if he pressed his temple to the glass and looked along the wall of the building he could see the eastern quarter narrowing into a line of stone chimneys and tile, and past them something that might have been green, at the edge where the rooftops ended and the city gave way to ground. Past the residential quarter, past the low buildings, past whatever else stood between this window and that field. He could not see it. The buildings between here and there hid everything he was looking for.

He stayed at the glass for a bit longer.

The food came and went. The glass emptied and filled. Someone was coming to the door while he slept. He never saw them. The bread was always warm. The apple was always a green apple. He ate the apples and left the rest.

On the fifth day, or what he believed was the fifth day, he got up. He went to the window and put his hand on the glass. Auralis was below him. Terracotta roofs catching the late light, the market square, the residential quarter where the flowering vines spilled from window boxes along the stone. He could see people from here. Small, purposeful, crossing between buildings the way people cross between buildings, with somewhere to go and the certainty that going there was worth it.

On the fifth day, or what he believed was the fifth day, he went to the window again and stayed.

He pressed his temple to the glass and looked east. The rooftops of the eastern quarter narrowing into chimneys and tile, and past them, at the edge where the city ended, something. He was sure of that. If he stood at a particular angle of the glass, he would see it. Green, or the thought of green. The Garden would be there. Past the residential quarter, past the low buildings along the canal. If the angle were different. If the glass were wider. If the rooftops between here and there would lower themselves by a foot he would see it. The wall. The gate. The field opening beyond the gate, the——

——The field opening beyond the gate. Blue. A low, dense blue that covered the ground the way water fills a basin, hundreds of small flowers growing so close their petals touched. Five petals each. Their centers were yellow and the yellow was very small against all that blue and the stems were thin and green and held their weight as though it cost them nothing. The blue went on past where he was standing to a tree line he could not place, and the air above it was warm and still and carried something at the edge of his breathing that thinned the further he reached for it.

His feet were in the flowers. They bent under his boots and straightened when he lifted his foot and the straightening was slow, patient, and when he looked back there was no path. The flowers had already filled the place where he had been.

Wind moved across the field and the blue shifted, every petal catching the light at a new angle, and the whole field changed color for a breath and changed back. He watched it happen again. The wind carried warmth, and something sweet, and something past the sweetness that had no name and would not stay.

He walked further. The tree line did not get closer. The flowers brushed his open palms as he moved through them and the touch was light.

At the edges the blue was thinning. The tree line was gone. The sky had paled to the color of the ceiling and the field was smaller now, the flowers less distinct, the petals losing their shape, becoming color only, becoming the idea of a color, and the air was cooling, the warmth leaving the way warmth leaves glass when the sun moves past it.

The glass was under his hand. The city. The rooftops of the eastern quarter narrowing into their line, and the green at the edge still there, still too far, still refusing to resolve into the thing he needed it to be.

His breathing was fast. He could feel his heartbeat in his throat, in his wrists, in the cracked rib that sent a low ache through his chest with each pulse.

He went back to the bed.

He lay on his back. Fourteen cracks. He counted them. The counting was rapid, his eyes jumping from crack to crack, and when he reached fourteen he started again and when he reached fourteen again he closed his eyes and the forget-me-nots were there immediately, pressing against the dark.

He opened his eyes and stared at the ceiling.

There was a morning when the light came through the window and he could not say whether it was the same morning as the last. He had been awake in the dark and then the dark had thinned and then the light was there and the ceiling was there and his body was there on the mattress, present, healing. The bandage was tighter. Cleaner. His rib moved less. At some point his shoulder closed the gap between thought and joint and he could not say when that had happened either.

The apple cores on the desk had been cleared. Four of them, gone, replaced by fresh bread. He did not remember eating four apples. He must have eaten them. The evidence was the absence.

He stood at the window again. He watched a woman cross the market square with a basket under her arm. She stopped at the bakery. She spoke to someone inside and came out with bread and folded it under the cloth and kept walking. He watched her until she turned a corner and the corner took her and the square was the same square it had been before she was in it.

He watched the square.

The square did nothing. People crossed it and the cobblestones held them and the awnings moved in a wind he could not feel from here and everything continued in the steady ordinary way that things continued, and he stood at the window fifteen floors above it with his hand on the glass and the glass was warm from the afternoon sun and the warmth went into his palm and stopped there.

He tried to remember the last time he had spoken to anyone. The door at the facility. Wen's jacket in his fists. The words he had said. The words Eli had said before that, standing across the console with the screens cycling between them, the shape on the floor of section twelve, the hum of the building holding them in place. Has it ever once occurred to you that you might simply be wrong.

He went back to the bed.

He found himself reaching for a bookshelf. The wall where it would have been was white plaster. His hand hung in the air, his fingers already curving around a spine, and he held them there for a moment before he let them drop.

This happened again near the door. He stopped at the handle as though waiting for the sound of someone on the other side, though the corridor was empty and the door was unlocked and had always been unlocked. His hand stayed on the handle. His thumb rested on the curve of the metal. Then he let go and walked back to the desk and sat down and put his hands flat on the surface and stayed there.

From the desk, if he turned his head, the window was behind him. If he turned the other way, the door. He did not turn his head. He sat with his hands on the wood and the glass of water within reach and the room held him there the way rooms hold people who have stopped choosing to leave them.

He was losing the order.

Morning, ceiling, food on the desk. Morning, ceiling, food on the desk. His shoulder worked. His rib was a dull ache. At some point he was healed enough that there was no reason to stay in the bed, and he stayed in the bed, and then he stopped staying in the bed and stood at the window instead, and the window showed him the same city and the same barrier at the edge and the same rooftops between here and the place where the Garden was.

Twenty-three. He had counted them. He counted them again.

He opened the window.

The air came through. Warm, carrying the smell of bread and stone and the flowering vines, the scent of Auralis in whatever month this was, and the city was loud from here. The market below him. Voices. Awnings. The sound of someone laughing, a bright quick sound that came up to him and touched something and he closed the window.

He opened the window.

He closed the window.

He stood with his forehead against the glass. Below him the market was shutting down, the awnings furling, the last merchants pulling their shutters with small distant bangs that came up to him one after another. His hands were on the sill. His knuckles went white by a shade. Two shades. The glass flexed under the pressure of his forehead, barely, a give so small it could have been the glass or his own bone bending.

He stepped back.

He went to the door. He put his hand on the handle. The handle was cool. The corridor beyond it would be lit and long and at the end of it the elevator, and the elevator would take him down, and the lobby would open onto the street, and the street would lead to the market, and the market would give way to the eastern quarter, and the eastern quarter would lead to the wall around the field, and past the wall.

His thumb traced the curve of the handle.

He locked the door.

It was a small lock. The kind that turns with a click barely louder than a breath. Maeve had not locked it. She had left it open the way you leave a door open when the person inside is free to go.

His hand on the lock.

He unlocked it. He locked it. He unlocked it.

He left it locked.

He went to the desk and sat down and put his hands flat on the wood and the glass of water was there and the window was behind him and the door was to his left and the lock was where he had left it, turned, and it was the only thing in this room he had moved from where it was since he awoke.

One of the mornings he woke facing the wall. This was new. The plaster had a texture up close that the white hid from a distance. A roughness, an unevenness, the small imperfections of a surface that someone had tried to make smooth and nearly succeeded. He put his hand against it. It was cool. The coolness went through his palm and stayed there, steady, and he kept his hand on the wall for a long time.

There was a day when he cleaned the room. He made the bed. He straightened the desk. He centered the glass. He folded the clothes someone had left and placed them in the drawer. He stood back and looked at it.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he unmade the bed. He pulled the sheets off and dropped them and lay on the bare mattress and the room looked different, as though the room itself had shifted.

He got up. He made the bed again. He unmade it. He made it. The sheets were wrinkled now, creased from being pulled and straightened and pulled, and the creases held the shape of his hands in them. He stood beside the bed and looked at the creases and his fingers were opening and closing at his sides, opening and closing, and he could not have said what they were reaching for.

He went to the window. East. The rooftops. He counted them and lost count at sixteen and started again and lost count at twelve. Started again. His lips were moving. He could feel them shaping the numbers without sound. Twenty-three. The number was the same. The number was always the same. Nothing about this city changed while he stood at this window. The rooftops stayed where they were. The barrier held its line.

The apple on the desk was untouched. He picked it up. He put it down. He picked it up. He held it in both hands and his thumbs pressed into the skin and he could feel the give of it, the flesh underneath yielding, and he pressed harder and the skin broke and the juice ran cold and sharp between his fingers and he stood there holding the broken thing and looking at it as though it had done something he had not expected.

He put it on the desk. He wiped his hands on his shirt. The juice left a stain and he looked at it and looked at the apple and his breathing had gone fast.

He sat on the floor. Back against the wall, beside the desk, on the cool tile. Knees up. Arms across them. His head was down and his hair fell forward, loose, the way it had been since what happened in the Control room.

He sat there. The light crossed the floor. The afternoon passed through the room. His breathing slowed. His hands went still.

Outside, the city went on being the city. The market, the voices, the distant sound of shutters opening or closing. The barrier at the edge, holding. The Garden surely was somewhere beyond the rooftops, growing, unchanged, asking nothing of him, waiting for nothing, simply there, the way a field is there, the way sunflowers turn toward the scorching sun like moths to a flame.

He sat on the floor with his head on his arms.

The desk had a drawer. Inside the drawer there was paper, blank, and a quill. He had opened the drawer on the second day, or the third, and seen them and closed the drawer. He had opened it again sometime later. He had touched the quill. He had closed the drawer.

He opened it now. He took out the paper. He took out the quill. He set them on the desk. He sat down.

The lamp was unlit. The barrier gave its light through the glass, it fell across the desk, and the paper, and the backs of his hands, and it was enough. Enough for him. He did not need to see.

He opened the notebook. The spine cracked. The first page was blank, the same white as the ceiling, the same white as the walls. He dipped the nib of the quill.

His handwriting was the handwriting he had always had. Careful. Vertical. The letters sat upright on the page the way his teacher had taught him, each one corrected until the script held itself without leaning. He wrote the date. He did not know the date. He wrote a number and crossed it out with a single clean line and left it.

He kept writing. A line, two lines. The logic building the way logic builds, one sentence following the next with the calm inevitability of a proof, each step narrowing the corridor until there was only one direction left. The handwriting was clean. The ink was even. He reached the bottom of the page and turned it and continued.

At some point the pen stopped.

He could not say when. He could say that the sentence he was writing had arrived at a word and the word was *therefore* and the word *therefore* required what came next to follow from what came before and what came before was that the path is fixed and what comes next is that nothing he does will change it and that was the proof and the proof was finished and the pen should have written the conclusion and the pen was resting on the paper and the ink was pooling at the point of contact, a dark circle spreading slowly into the grain.

*Therefore*.

He had written this proof before. He had finished it before. The conclusion was always the same. The conclusion had been the same since the first time he put pen to paper in a room that was not this room and wrote the thing he knew to be true and arrived at the end of it and set the pen down and the knowing had not helped.

He drew a line through the page. One long horizontal stroke, nib pressing deep, the ink scoring across every sentence he had written. He turned the sheet over.

He pulled a fresh page from the drawer.

He did not write the proof again. He wrote something else. He wrote a question, and the question was not in his careful vertical script. It leaned. It came out leaning, as though the hand that wrote it had already decided it was not going to survive. He looked at it. He crossed it out. He could hear it. That was the problem. He could hear the question in a voice that was not his, in the exact register it had been asked, quiet and steady, in a room with screens and a body and the hum of a building about to die.
