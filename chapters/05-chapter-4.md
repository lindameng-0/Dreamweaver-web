The first thing Eli did was gather the papers.

He moved through the room on his knees, pulling sheets from the carpet, from under the overturned coffee table, from the places where they had settled against the bodies. Some of them came away clean. Others had soaked through, the ink running where the blood had reached it, and those he handled with more care, pinching them at their dry corners the way you might lift a leaf you didn't want to tear. He read none of them. He had already read enough.

The fireplace still held its weak flame from the night before. He fed the clean pages in first and watched the edges curl and blacken. The blood-soaked ones he laid on top, and they sat there for a long time, steaming, refusing to catch. He knelt by the grate and waited. The wet paper darkened and bubbled and the smell that came off it was not paper and not blood but something in between, something that stuck to the back of his throat. Eventually the flame found its way through. He stayed until every page had gone to ash and the ash had gone to nothing, and then he stayed a little longer.

When he stood and turned, Luca was gone from the window. Eli could hear the faint creak of floorboards from the study, the sound of someone settling into a space they had chosen for themselves. He did not go to check.

The squad leader was closest. Eli crouched beside him and looked at his face. The man's eyes were still open and his mouth was still open and the second half of his sentence was still somewhere in his throat where it would stay. Eli closed the man's eyes with his thumb and forefinger. He straightened the collar where it had bunched during the fall. Then he slid his hands beneath the man's shoulders and moved him.

It would have been faster with the threads. He used them anyway, but only to open the door, and to lay the man down gently once he reached the corridor. The rest was his hands and his arms and the slow, careful work of not dragging, not pulling, but carrying each one to the door and then through it and then setting them down against the corridor wall with a kind of attention that had no audience and needed none.

He did this seven times. The operative with the hole in his chest was the heaviest, or felt that way. Eli arranged them in a row. He did not line them up with any particular precision but he made sure none of them were lying on top of each other, and he closed the eyes of the ones whose eyes were open, and when one man's arm fell at an angle that looked uncomfortable Eli moved it, though he knew it did not matter, though he knew the man could not feel it and would never feel anything again.

His hands were red when he finished. He washed them in the kitchen sink and watched the water run pink and then clear.

The room was still a ruin. Shattered glass from the coffee table. The painting hanging by one corner. Blood everywhere, soaked deep into the carpet in overlapping pools that had merged into a single dark shape. The perfume from the broken bottle on the shelf had mixed with the cordite and the copper smell, and the result was something sweet and wrong that would not leave.

He picked the glass out of the carpet by hand. He righted the painting and found that the frame had cracked along one side, so he took it down and leaned it against the wall. He gathered the ceramic pieces from the cups and saucers into a pile. The carpet was beyond him. He stood over it for a while, looking at the stain, and then he left it.

It was perhaps an hour before the knock came. Two men from maintenance and a woman who wore the logistics insignia on her sleeve. They had a cart with them. One of the men had already seen the corridor.

"General." The woman's voice was careful. "We were notified of a containment situation on this floor."

"All handled." Eli opened the door wide enough for them to see the room. The glass was cleared. The furniture was upright. The carpet was the carpet. "I could use a replacement, though." He nodded toward the floor. "If you have one."

She looked past him. Her eyes moved across the stain and then came back to Eli's face. Whatever she was thinking stayed behind her expression.

"We'll need to process the room, General. Standard protocol for any breach event."

"Of course. I've preserved what's relevant. The bodies are in the corridor for your team." He paused. "I'm sorry about the state of things. I should have left it for you. I just didn't want anyone stepping on the glass."

The woman looked at the two men behind her. Something passed between them, the kind of calculation that happens when a maintenance crew is standing in front of a general who has already done their job for them and is apologizing for it.

"We have a standard-issue carpet on the cart," she said. "Same dimensions as the residential units on this floor."

"Would it be all right if I laid it myself?" Eli asked. "You've already got your hands full with the corridor. I don't want to add to it."

A pause. She studied him.

"You'll need to submit an incident report," she said. "General Sylvia's office. Before end of day."

"Of course."

"We'll process the remains." She turned to go, then stopped. "General. For the record. You should have waited for us."

"You're right," Eli said. "I should have. I'm sorry."

They left. Eli stood in the doorway holding the carpet roll and listened to their footsteps fade. Then he carried it inside, set it on the floor, and began the work of pulling up the old one. The bloodstain had seeped through to the concrete beneath. He looked at it. A map of something that had happened and would need to never have happened, at least not the way it did. He unrolled the new carpet over it and smoothed the edges with his palms.

From the study came the sound of a chair shifting. Then silence.

Eli washed his hands again before he entered his study. Luca was at the window. He stood with his back to the room and the morning light falling across the floor in front of him, and he did not turn when Eli came in.

Eli sat down at his desk. He pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him and began to write.

He wrote the first line three times. He crossed out the first two and left the third, then crossed that out too.

The pen was the problem. He'd picked up the good one, the one with the brass nib that caught the light in a way he liked, and it wrote too well. Every sentence came out looking like it mattered. He put it back and took the other pen from the drawer, the one whose ink came out thin and pale and had to be coaxed along with pressure.

The report was for Sylvia. Or rather, it was for the six or seven intelligence analysts Sylvia would hand it to before she did anything official. Eli had met a few of them over the years. They were the kind of people who noticed when a timestamp said 03:00 but the patrol log said 02:47. They underlined things. They kept rulers on their desks for underlining. Sylvia herself had already made up her mind, Eli was fairly sure of that, but Sylvia, like anyone else, was constrained by protocol. The analysts had to sign off before she could act, and analysts who found nothing wrong with a report were analysts who couldn't justify an escalation.

He tried the straightforward approach first. At 03:00, a six-person team breached the eastern stairwell. He stopped. He didn't actually know if they'd used the eastern stairwell. He'd been at the barrier when it happened, chasing a false alarm. By the time he returned, the squad was dead and the intelligence officer was dead and the person who killed them all was standing in the middle of it looking at him, and the specific question of which stairwell they'd used had not seemed important to think about at the time.

It still didn't seem important. What seemed important was that six people had come to take someone, and that someone was standing at the window right now, looking out, and the report Eli was writing would determine whether that someone lived or died. That was the shape of the problem. The shape was clear. It was the details that kept refusing to cooperate.

He wrote a paragraph about the squad's probable objective. It was, he felt, a convincing paragraph. It placed the squad's target as the signals intelligence office, which was plausible, because signals had been intercepting Citadel communications for months. The paragraph had a logical flow to it that Eli found satisfying. He read it back to himself and liked the way it moved from premise to conclusion with the steady inevitability of water running downhill.

"No one will believe that," Luca said from the window.

Eli's pen stayed where it was.

"Signals is on the tenth floor." Luca did not look away from the window. "You live on the thirtieth. You've written a squad that broke into one of the most fortified buildings in Auralis and then walked past the thing they came for. Twenty floors past it."

Eli looked at that paragraph. It really was a good paragraph. The sentences were balanced. The reasoning was elegant. It was also, now that Luca mentioned it, describing a squad that had apparently breached one of the most heavily guarded buildings in Auralis, bypassed five floors of military offices, climbed past the signals division entirely, and continued upward to a residential floor for no discernible reason.

He drew a line through the whole thing.

He started again. This time he wrote about Patel.

Patel was the intelligence officer. Had been. Eli wrote about Patel's work, his decryption access, his role in an ongoing signals operation. He built a version of the night in which Patel had come to the thirtieth floor to brief Eli on intercepted communications, and the extraction team had followed him there, and everything that happened after was an intelligence grab gone wrong. It explained the floor. It explained the bodies. It gave the whole thing a shape that made sense from the outside.

He was halfway through the second paragraph when he noticed what he was doing. He had given Patel a reason for being in the building that night. Then he had given Patel a reason for caring about his work. Then he had written a sentence about the man's conviction that the decryption project could shorten the war, and that sentence had a quality to it, the kind of sentence that makes people go quiet when it's read aloud.

Eli sat back. He had met Patel twice. Both times they'd talked about weather. He didn't know if Patel had convictions. He didn't know if Patel liked his job or was counting days until he could transfer out. But Eli's pen had kept moving, and now there was a man on the page who believed in something and died for it, and the whole paragraph had the cadence of something you'd hear at a memorial, and Sylvia's analysts would read it and feel nothing at all except the urge to check the eighth-floor access logs, where they would find that Patel had not swiped in that evening.

"You're writing a eulogy," Luca said. He had not moved from the window. "For a man you spoke to twice."

Eli didn't ask how Luca knew he'd only met Patel twice. Luca knew things. That was the fundamental condition of being in a room with him.

"You're trying to give them a story," Luca continued, his gaze still fixed on whatever held it beyond the glass. "Don't. Sylvia needs her analysts to find something worth flagging. If you give them a story, they'll pick it apart. Give them nothing. A report so empty it's not worth the paper it takes to flag."

Eli looked at the page. Patel's quiet conviction looked back at him from a paragraph that was three-quarters fiction and one-quarter weather.

He crossed everything out and began again.

This time he wrote the way you fill out a form. Time of breach, approximate. Squad composition, six, observed from remains. Casualty, one, identified as Analyst Patel, cause of death under review. Situation contained. He wrote sentences that opened no doors. No narrative. No motive. No reason for the squad to have been there and no reason for them to be dead and no mention of the person who had killed them. Each sentence ended and the next one began and there was nothing between them that a careful reader could grip.

He pushed the page to the edge of the desk.

Ten minutes later Luca passed by on his way to the kitchen. He picked up the report and read it standing. His eyes moved down the page once. He set it back.

"Third paragraph. 'Consequently.' You don't use that word. None of your other reports do." He took a sip. " 'Which meant' will do."

"You've read my other reports."

"You keep them on open shelves." Luca was already turning back toward the window. "In the room where I sleep."

Eli changed the word. He read the report once more. It was nothing. It said nothing about Patel, nothing about why six people had broken into his quarters, nothing about the young man standing ten feet away who had killed them all and then tossed a gun onto the carpet and smiled. It was empty and it was hollow and it might, if he was lucky, bore Sylvia's analysts into signing it without comment.

He folded the paper once and set it aside. His thumb was smeared with ink. He rubbed it against his shirt and it didn't come off. He rubbed harder. It smeared.

Across the room, Luca turned another page.

Eli sat there for a while. He listened to the pages turn and to the building waking up around them, the distant sound of boots in corridors, a door closing somewhere below, the elevator humming in its shaft. Morning had come all the way into the room now. The thin line of light on the carpet had widened into a broad pale band that reached the foot of Luca's armchair. Luca did not seem to notice. He read the way he did everything, with the absolute attention of someone who had nowhere else to be and no interest in being anywhere else.

"I'm going to the debrief," he said to the room.

Luca did not look up. "I know."

Eli found a folder in the bottom drawer, slid the paper inside, and stood. His legs were stiff. He didn't know how long he'd been sitting. Eli washed his hands in the kitchen sink for the third time that morning. The ink faded but didn't disappear. He dried his hands on a cloth and looked at them and decided they would have to do. He took one last look at the new carpet in the living room. It was a shade lighter than the one it replaced.

"If someone comes to the door," Eli said, "don't answer it."

"I don't answer doors."

The corridor was busy. Staff moved through it in the quick purposeful way that followed any breach event, carrying equipment, relaying orders, not stopping to talk. Eli nodded to a few of them as he passed. He took the stairs because the elevator would be crowded and because the stairs gave him four flights to organize his face into the right arrangement. By the second landing he had it. By the third he believed it. By the time he reached the conference level he was just Eli, unhurried, a little tired, carrying a folder that looked like every other folder.

He could hear the meeting room through the door. That low communal hum of voices that hadn't yet found a subject, the sound of people waiting to be told what had happened to them. He added his report to the file on the side table without pausing. It sat between a perimeter assessment and a maintenance log, thin and plain, one copy among dozens.

He took his seat. The morning was doing what mornings did in this room, pouring through the floor-to-ceiling windows and laying itself across the polished wood in long warm strips. Dust turned slowly in the light. Someone had placed a fresh carafe of water at the center of the table, and the glass caught the sun and threw a small trembling oval onto the ceiling that no one was looking at. Rae sat at the head with her hands folded. Emerson was reviewing a document. Two junior generals Eli rarely spoke to were murmuring near the far end. Sylvia stood by the window with her arms crossed. She had not sat down. She would not sit down.

Maeve arrived last. She came through the door the way she always came through doors, as though the room had been waiting for her specifically and she was sorry to have kept it. Her heels were quiet on the carpet. She took her seat, crossed one leg over the other, and set her tablet on the table with a sound so soft it was almost a courtesy.

"Shall we begin?"

She opened the debrief without hurry. Her voice carried the particular warmth she reserved for rooms full of people who needed to feel that someone capable was in charge.

"At approximately 3 AM, a Citadel sabotage team breached the Skyscraper through the eastern perimeter. Their apparent target was the signals intelligence division on the tenth floor. General Eli engaged the team on the thirtieth floor and neutralized the threat. One Auralis intelligence officer, Analyst Patel, was killed during the confrontation." She paused to take a sip of water. "The breach point has been sealed. Structural damage is being assessed. The relevant incident reports are in the file."

She set the glass down and touched the corner of her tablet with one fingernail.

"Unless there are pressing questions, I'd like to move to the matter of barrier reinforcement. The fact that a Citadel team reached the interior at all suggests vulnerabilities we haven't yet mapped."

The room settled into the particular quiet of people preparing to discuss logistics. Papers shuffled. Someone uncapped a pen. Eli let himself lean back in his chair. The carafe on the table looked like a small glass building from here, catching the morning in its walls. Rae was pulling a folder from the stack in front of her. Emerson cleared his throat and said something about reinforcement schedules. The conversation turned to patrol rotations, resource allocation, whether the breach indicated a systematic weakness or an isolated probe. Familiar numbers. The kind this room had discussed a hundred times.

"Before we move on." Sylvia's voice landed in the middle of the logistics discussion without apology. Her arms were still crossed. "General Eli's report states he secured the scene before the investigative crew arrived. He filed the incident summary himself. There's no entry-point data, no stairwell confirmation, no access log cross-reference." She looked at Eli. "Why did you clean the room?"

The quiet that followed was a different kind of quiet. Not hostile. Procedural. Sylvia asked hard questions because Sylvia always asked hard questions, and the room knew this about her the way they knew the sun came through these windows every morning. But Eli could feel people recalibrating, that small collective shift when a conversation changes direction.

"I did," he said. He leaned forward, resting his forearms on the table, and let his hands open. "And you're right to ask. I came back from the barrier and found the squad already down and the room in pieces. Glass everywhere, the coffee table shattered, Patel was on the floor." He paused. "It was four in the morning. I wasn't thinking about protocol. I was thinking about the first person who would walk through that door and what they'd have to step through to get to him. I should have sealed the room and waited for your team. I didn't. I'm sorry."

He looked at Sylvia.

"Whatever you need from me to verify the account, you'll have it. I mean that."

Sylvia studied him for a moment. She opened her mouth to say something, but she didn’t. She simply turned back to the window. That was all. Rae resumed her folder. Emerson picked up where he'd left off. Maeve steered the discussion back to barrier reinforcement with the easy authority of a hand returning to a familiar wheel, and the meeting became what meetings were, people speaking in turns about things they understood, voices overlapping, pens moving across paper.

Eli listened. He made a note on the margin of his briefing copy. Someone refilled the carafe. The oval of light on the ceiling had drifted an inch to the left, crawling toward the far wall the way it did every morning at this hour. Maeve was asking Rae about the western watch rotation. Rae was answering. The numbers were soothing in their familiarity, troop counts and shift lengths and supply lines, and the sound of it was the sound of people doing what they knew how to do.

Sylvia was still standing by the window. She had uncrossed her arms. Her fingers rested on the sill, drumming a pattern that Eli recognized as impatience. She was waiting for the logistics to end. She was waiting for the part of the meeting where someone would finally say the word she had been carrying in her mouth since she walked in.

Retaliation.

The carafe's light on the ceiling had faded to a pale smudge. The sun was moving past the windows. Maeve was asking about supply chain redundancies. Emerson was answering.

Then the glass behind Sylvia made a sound.

Small. A tap. Almost delicate, like a fingernail against crystal. Sylvia's hand stilled on the sill. Eli's head turned. He saw the hole appear in the upper left corner of the pane, a single bright point no larger than a coin, and for one fraction of a second it looked like a star that had gotten lost and pressed itself against the wrong side of the sky.

The cracks spread faster than he could track. They raced out from that point in every direction, a web that covered the entire pane in the time it took to draw a single breath, and then the glass let go.

It fell. That was what Eli would remember afterward. It didn't explode. It fell, thousands of pieces releasing at once, a shimmering curtain that poured across the table and the floor and the shoulders of everyone who hadn't moved yet. Sylvia threw herself sideways from the sill. The sound was almost beautiful. Almost like rain on a roof very far away.

Then the wind hit.

Papers tore from the table and scattered. The carafe toppled and water spread across the wood in a thin fast sheet that reached the edge and spilled.

Through the ruined frame came a shape. Grey, compact, trailing fragments that caught the light in ways glass didn't. Purple shards that dissolved as they fell. Pieces of the barrier. Eli watched one land on the back of his hand. It looked like nothing. And then it was nothing.

The drone stopped above the center of the table. It hovered there, perfectly still, and the room around it had gone so quiet that Eli could hear the water from the carafe dripping off the edge onto the floor.

Drip. Drip.

A seam opened along the drone's underside. Light spilled out. A screen assembled itself from the light, flat and grey and hanging in the air where the oval of sun had been a moment ago. It only showed static.

The voice that came through had been stripped clean. Pitch. Breath. Warmth.

"Auralis Command."

Nobody moved. Maeve's hand rested on her cracked tablet. Rae's fingers had found her ring. Sylvia's hand was at her hip where a weapon should have been, and Eli watched her fingers close on nothing.

"Seventy-two hours ago, Citadel dispatched an extraction team to your facility. Your official account classifies this as a sabotage operation targeting your signals intelligence division."

A pause. The static pulsed.

"This is incorrect. The team's objective was the retrieval of a defected Citadel asset. Designation: the Prophet."

Eli's hands were flat on the table. He could feel the water soaking into his sleeves.

"The extraction team was killed inside the personal quarters of General Eli. Thirtieth floor, eastern wing. General Eli was not present for the engagement. He was drawn to the barrier perimeter by a diversionary breach." The voice gave no emphasis to any of this. It said Eli's name the way it would say a grid coordinate. "The Prophet killed the extraction team. All six operatives. He is currently inside your city."

Somewhere to his left, someone was breathing too fast.

"The device you are observing penetrated your barrier seventeen seconds ago. This is a demonstration of capability."

The water dripped from the table. Once. Twice.

"You have five days. Locate the Prophet. Execute him. If at the end of five days the Prophet remains alive inside Auralis, Citadel will treat your government as an actor harboring a defected intelligence asset and will respond accordingly."

The static settled into something dense. Flat. Like the air before lightning.

"Your barrier will not hold."

The screen went dark. The drone hung in silence for one more second. Eli saw the seam widen. The light that came through was white. Too white. The kind of bright that leaves shapes on the inside of your eyelids. He was already moving when it went.

The sound was everywhere. Eli felt it in his chest before he heard it, a pressure that shoved the air out of his lungs. Two generals went into the wall. Smoke erupted from the center of the table in a thick column that hit the ceiling and spread outward, swallowing the room from the top down. Something metallic struck the far wall and rang. Debris scattered across the wood, sharp and hot, hissing where it landed in the pooled water.

Eli's ears rang. The ringing was distant, as though it belonged to a room he'd already left. Ash settled on his shoulders. Glass dust covered his hands. The air tasted of burnt metal and something chemical and sweet, and for a moment he was in two rooms at once, this one and the one from two nights ago, the blood and the smoke and the scattered papers, and he could not tell them apart.

The smoke thinned. Where the drone had been there was a hole in the table. Its edges glowed faintly orange. The carafe was gone. The water had turned grey with ash. The oval of light on the ceiling was gone too. Nothing caught the sun now.

Eli looked around the room. Generals were getting to their feet, coughing, wiping glass from their faces. Emerson pressed his hand to a cut above his eyebrow and the blood ran between his fingers. Maeve sat in her chair. She had not moved. She was looking at the crack across her tablet screen with the expression of a woman whose evening has been very slightly inconvenienced.

Sylvia moved.

She crossed the room in three strides. Her hands hit the table's edge and she swung herself up and her boots cracked against the wood and she walked straight to the scorched hole at the center and crouched and picked up a shard of the drone's casing with her bare hand and stood with it in her fist.

"Five days." She turned the shard over. She was not looking at anyone. She was looking at the hole. "Five days and they think we'll roll over. They think they can fly a bomb into our meeting room and give us orders." Her fist closed around the shard until something cracked. Blood welled between her fingers. She didn't look at it. "I want a strike force assembled within the hour. Full eastern deployment. If they want to give us five days, we give them five days they'll remember."

No one spoke. Maeve's hand rested on her cracked tablet. She did not object.

Sylvia turned on the table, glass grinding under her heels. Her eyes found Eli.

The fury that had been pointed outward, at the drone, at the static voice and its countdown, turned. It turned the way a searchlight turns, and when it found him it stayed.

"But first." Her voice dropped. Quieter now, which was worse. "They said something else. They said the squad came for the Prophet. You said they came for Patel. They said you weren't even in the room. You said you engaged and neutralized the threat." She held up the shard. Blood ran along its edge. "I want your account to be true, Eli. Do you understand me? I want you to be right. Because if your report holds, then Citadel just lied to a room full of generals, and that gives me everything I need. Four years I've waited for a justification to mobilize. Four years. And if you are telling the truth, I have it."

She stepped forward. The table groaned.

"But if you are not telling the truth." She pointed the shard at him. Her hand had stopped shaking. That was the worst part. "If you cleaned that room because there was something in it you didn't want found. If you wrote that report to protect someone."

She leaned in. Close enough that Eli could see the glass dust in her hair, the sweat at her temples, the pulse at the side of her throat.

"Then you have stolen my war from me. And I will take it out of you personally."

She did not blink.

"Do we understand each other?"

Eli stood. Slowly. He did not step back from the shard in her hand.

"Sylvia," he said. His voice was quiet. "Look at this room."

She did not look.

"Look at it," he said again, gently. "The glass. The smoke. Emerson is bleeding. Rae can barely hear. A machine just flew through our barrier and detonated at our table." He paused. "This is what Citadel wanted. This room. This exact room. Every person who was sitting here ten minutes ago trusted each other. We trusted each other. And now we are standing in smoke and broken glass and you are holding a weapon and pointing it at me."

He took a breath.

"I have known you for four years. You are the most honest person in this building. If you tell me that my word means nothing, then I have failed in a way that matters far more to me than whatever Citadel claims. But I am asking you to consider something." He let the silence hold for a moment. "The people who sent that drone did not send it so that we would find the truth. They sent it so that we would stop looking for it together."

Sylvia's hand trembled. The shard did not lower.

"That is a very moving answer," she said, "to a very simple question."

"Sylvia." Maeve's voice came from behind them. She rose from her chair. She picked a sliver of glass from her cuff and placed it on the table with a small precise click. "You're bleeding, darling. Do sit down."

Sylvia did not sit down.

"We can discuss the strike," Maeve continued. Her voice carried the particular patience of a woman who has never had to raise it. "And we will discuss the strike. But we will not discuss it while pointing sharp things at our own people based on a broadcast from the enemy that just bombed us. Whatever Citadel said in that transmission was designed for exactly this." She looked around the room, meeting each pair of eyes, unhurried, the way a hostess makes sure every guest has been attended to. "I will not do their work for them. I hope none of you will either."

The room shifted. The weight of it moved toward Maeve's composure, the way a current pulls toward deeper water.

Sylvia stepped down from the table. Glass ground beneath her heel. The blood fell from her fist to the floor.

"Then verify it." The words came through her teeth. "Send the reconstruction specialist to his quarters. Now. Today. If Eli's account holds, Citadel is lying, and I lead the strike myself. Tonight. I will not wait another day."

She turned to Maeve. Her eyes were bright and wet and the wet had nothing to do with grief.

"And if it doesn't hold, Maeve, then all your grace in the world won't help him."

"Standard protocol," Maeve said. She might have been confirming a dinner reservation. "Conflicting accounts require forensic verification. The specialist will examine the quarters within the hour." She turned to Eli. The crack in her tablet caught the light and split it. "That is acceptable, I trust?"

"Of course," Eli said.

Sylvia looked at him one last time. She set the shard of casing on the table next to the drops of her own blood, carefully, with the deliberateness of someone putting down a weapon they have decided not to use yet. She turned and walked out. The door did not slam. She closed it behind her with a quietness that was worse than any slam, and the sound of her boots carried down the corridor for a long time after.

People left in ones and twos. They stepped around the glass and did not speak. Someone righted an overturned chair. Someone else began to gather the scattered papers and stopped halfway and put them back down and left instead. Rae paused at the door. She looked at Eli across the ruined room. Her hand found her ring and turned it once. Her mouth opened. It closed. She left.

The wind came through the broken window in gusts, lifting the ash from the table and setting it down. The scorch mark at the center had cooled to black. From far below came the sound of the city, the market and the merchants and the awnings, and from this height and through this smoke it sounded like something happening to someone else.

Maeve was the last to stand. She gathered her tablet and held it against her chest, and walked toward the door. Her heels were quiet on the glass. She stopped beside Eli's chair. She did not touch him. She looked at the table. At the ash. At the dark ring on the wood where his copy of the report had been.

"One hour," she said. "Do make sure the room is presentable."

She smoothed a crease on her sleeve and left.

Eli sat alone. The wind stirred the ash and moved the papers on the floor and touched his face with the cold of the morning. He could still taste the smoke. He could still hear the voice, flat and emptied of everything, telling the room what he had done.

He looked down at his hands. The ink smudge was still there on his thumb. Through everything, the smoke and the glass and the explosion, it was still there.

One hour.

He stood. Glass shifted under his feet. The oval of light was gone. The sun had moved past the glass that was no longer there. What he stood in was just a room now, with a hole in its table and ash on its floor and a smell that would take days to leave.

When he walked out, the door swung shut behind him and the wind pushed it open again and he did not go back to close it. There was no time. There was barely enough time at all.

He made it to the end of the corridor before his pace changed. He was taking steps three at a time, and then the thirtieth-floor window was open and he was through it and flying close to the building with his wing tips brushing glass. He counted windows. His own was still open. He went through sideways, one shoulder first, and his feet found the floor and the quiet of his quarters closed around him.

Luca was in the armchair. He was staring at the floor. His hands were on his knees, his body so still he could have been asleep with his eyes open. When Eli came through the window instead of the door, Luca's gaze lifted a fraction. It registered the glass dust. The window. Then it settled back.

"We have a problem," Eli said. He was still by the window. His breathing hadn't leveled. "A drone came through the barrier during the debrief. Citadel's leader. He told the entire room the extraction squad wasn't sabotage. They were sent for you."

Luca looked at him the way he looked at everything.

"They know you're here. They know you killed the squad. They know I wasn't in the room." Eli's hand went to the windowsill behind him. He could feel the cold of it through his palm. "Every general in Auralis just heard Citadel say that my report is a lie. Five days to find you and hand you over or they bring the barrier down."

He watched Luca's face.

"You are now an enemy of Auralis and an enemy of Citadel simultaneously. There is no third place in this world, Luca."

The same flat gaze. The same stillness that could have been armor or vacancy or something else entirely that Eli did not have time to decode.

"I heard the explosion from here," Luca said.

He looked back at the floor.

Eli let five seconds pass. Six. Seven. He pushed off from the windowsill.

"Sylvia demanded a scene reconstruction. Someone is coming to this room within the hour. A specialist who reads movement patterns, ability residue, the physical sequence of what happened." He crossed the room. He stopped in front of the armchair. "She will see that someone else killed that squad. And then they will know I lied, Luca. Every general in this building. They will know I wrote a false report and signed my name to it and they will want to know why."

Something shifted in Luca's gaze. Small. A recalculation, maybe, behind those flat eyes. Or maybe nothing at all.

"So what are you going to do about it?"

"I need you gone before they arrive. Market, corridor, anywhere."

"And if I'd rather stay?"

"I need you to go. Just for a little while."

"And if I'd rather stay?"

Eli crouched in front of the armchair. He rested his forearms on his knees, bringing himself to Luca's eye level, and smiled the way he smiled at frightened soldiers, at children who couldn't sleep, at anyone who needed to believe that the person in front of them had everything under control.

"You could," he said. "But I'd have to explain you to Sylvia, and I'm not sure I have enough words for that. I'm certainly not sure I have enough words to explain why I lied for you." His voice was light. "And if I can't explain it, then they take you and they take me, and I think you know which one of those I mind more."

Luca's gaze, which had been flat and settled and indifferent to everything Eli had said for the past three minutes, moved to Eli's face and stayed there.

"Go for a walk," Eli said. "Get an apple from the market. It’s nice out at this time of day."

Luca's eyes held his for a long moment. Then he stood in one motion and walked to the door. His hand found the handle. He paused there. Then he opened the door and pulled it shut behind him and the sound was barely a sound at all.

Eli stood alone. Morning light on the new carpet. Dust turning in the air. Everything peaceful and useless.

Less than an hour.

He raised his hands.

The threads came from his fingertips the way they always did, thin and gold, almost weightless. He opened his palms and let them go, and they poured from him like something released, streaming outward in every direction, up toward the ceiling, down toward the floor, out toward the walls, each one trailing its own faint light.

He let more come. And more. The threads multiplied, splitting and splitting again, finer than hair, finer than that, until the air was full of them, a thousand gold lines radiating from where he stood, reaching for every corner of the room. They found the walls and kept going, pressing into plaster, into wood, sinking through the new carpet and into the concrete beneath. Eli stood at the center of it with his arms open and his breathing easy and his face carrying no particular expression.

The room filled with light, the way the last hour before sunset is soft, the gold of it coming from everywhere at once, from the threads themselves, each one carrying its own small glow. The dust that had been turning in the morning sun was turning in gold now. Eli's shadow was gone. There were no shadows anywhere. The threads had reached every place a shadow could form and replaced it with that same gentle warmth, and the air itself had taken on a color, amber, the amber of late summer, of fields, of something he knew by heart.

He let the threads keep coming. They filled the space beneath the desk. They lined the inside of the fireplace. They settled on the cold tea in yesterday's cup like a skin of gold. And beneath them, under all of it, Luca's traces and the squad's traces and the gunfire and the blood sank and sank, the way stones disappear when water rises high enough.

Somewhere in the building Rae would be feeling this. The draw passing through her the way all draws did, because she was the source and there was no other source, and she would feel the volume of it and she would know. There was no ambiguity in what she would feel. There was no possibility that she would mistake this for standard use or combat residue or anything other than exactly what it was, which was General Eli pulling enough energy to saturate an entire residential floor at the precise moment a reconstruction team was being assembled to examine it. And she would carry that knowledge with her into whatever came next, and there was nothing Eli could do about that, and he had known this before he raised his hands, and he had raised them anyway.

He stood there with his arms out and his palms open and a thousand threads of light streaming from his hands into every corner of the only room in Auralis that could not be allowed to tell the truth. And somewhere in the middle of it his breathing slowed.

He lowered his hands.

The threads farthest from him vanished first. The ones along the bookshelves followed, and the window, and the desk, fading in the order they'd arrived, gold giving way to amber giving way to the ordinary morning.

Eli filled the kettle. Set it on the stove. The flame caught on the first try.

He washed his face in the sink and the water was cool against his skin and he dried it with the cloth that hung from the hook by the window and folded the cloth and put it back. The kettle made its small sounds. Metal expanding. The first whisper of heat finding water. He stood and listened to it the way you listen to rain when you have nowhere to be. When it whistled he made tea. He carried the cup to his desk and sat down and the warmth of the ceramic settled into his palms and the room smelled of tea leaves.

He opened a book. He did not read it.

The last threads dissolved around him as the minutes passed. A strand clinging to the lamp thinned to nothing as he turned a page. Another, draped across the armchair's back, lost its glow and was gone. The room became a room again. The morning light was just morning light. The carpet was just carpet.

The knock came as the last sweetness left the air. He set the cup down.

"Come in."

The specialist entered first. Small, close-cropped hair, a face built for a kind of attention most people never used. Behind her, two officers with clipboards. Behind them, Sylvia.

Sylvia did not come in. She leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, watching the specialist and Eli in the same way a hawk watches the ground—patient only because the kill requires it.

"General." The specialist's voice was professional. "With your permission."

"Of course." He gestured with his cup. "Anywhere you need."

She stepped inside and stopped. She stood very still. Her eyes moved across the room but they were reading something underneath the surfaces, the way a person watches a river for the current beneath.

Her brow creased.

She moved through the living room slowly. Her hands rose occasionally to touch the air beside a wall, above the carpet, over the spot where the coffee table had been. Her fingers spread there, two feet above the floor, pressing against something no one else could feel.

From the doorway, Sylvia's fingers drummed against her arm. *One two three four. One two three four.*

The specialist moved to the bookshelf. The desk. The window. At each place the same crease, the same focused distance. The officers behind her wrote in quick scratches.

She returned to the center of the room. The exact center, where luca stood, where the papers flew. She closed her eyes.

Eli took a sip of tea.

She opened her eyes. She turned to Sylvia.

"General Eli's energy signature is present throughout the room at very high density. Consistent with sustained, high-intensity ability deployment." A pause. Her gaze flickered to Eli and back. "The saturation makes detailed movement reconstruction difficult. Underlying event traces are largely obscured."

Sylvia's drumming stopped.

"The overall energy profile does not contradict the general's filed report."

*Does not contradict.* The distance between that phrase and confirmed was the distance between a door closed and a door locked. Sylvia stood in the doorway and Eli could see her considering those words over and over in the way she was staring squarely at him.

Her arms uncrossed. Her hand went to her pocket and came out holding a shard of the drone's casing. She turned it between her fingers.

She looked at the specialist. At the shard. At Eli.

She dropped the fragment. It hit the floor near the carpet's edge and broke into three pieces.

Her earpiece crackled. Her gaze moved past Eli. Past the window. Past the barrier and the wasteland, toward something too far away to see. "So the Citadel bastards are liars after all." She bit down on the words. "Good. That's all I needed."

She pushed off the doorframe and was gone. The officers followed. One paused over the broken shard, thought better of it, and left it where it lay.

The specialist stayed. She stood in the center of the room with her hands at her sides and looked at Eli, and then she looked at the air around her, at whatever she could see that he could not. Then she too had left the room.
