title: Applause Like Snow
vigil: VIGIL II — THE DESCENT
folio: 89

The Theatre does not begin with walls. It begins with acoustics. The dark around the falling weaver acquired, by degrees, the particular hush of an auditorium — that upholstered silence with a thousand small attentions inside it — and then there was carpet under his boots, and an aisle sloping down between rows he could not see the ends of, and a stage lit by nothing, brilliantly.

The seats were occupied. He had known they would be and it helped not at all. The Audience does not move as one; that is the horror people get wrong when they retell it. They shift singly, like any crowd — a crossed leg here, a tilted head there — each silhouette cut from the dark between stage lights and dressed in it, and every face turned toward the stage with an expression that cannot be seen but can be felt, the way you feel a window behind you.

On the stage, a figure in dark clothes reached upward, and grief happened to him beautifully. Riku stood in the aisle and watched Sayo Kurose's nightmare from the inside: the reaching figure, the applause building somewhere ahead of the gesture like weather. Whoever performed it knew the grief exactly — the abandoned braking gesture of the hands, the way the reach went on past hope into habit. He had mended four hundred nightmares. He had never seen one acted with love.

“You're standing,” said a voice at his elbow, conversationally. “There's a seat.”

The Prompter was below stage level, in the little hooded box the old theatres kept, and he was looking up at Riku with eyes that reflected the stage the way still water reflects a window at night. Dark hair, dark coat, a chain of tarnished silver, and a voice pitched for exactly one listener at a time. “You've come about the woman who does not dream,” he said. “Eleventh row. She's here every night. She's here now. That is rather the difficulty with your contract, weaver — you cannot mend a dream that is not a dream. This is a performance. It has a rights-holder.”

Riku had questions in the queue ahead of that one, but the Prompter tilted his head, and the whisper came up out of the box like a draught under a door: “You dropped a line, eight years ago. You have been playing the scene without it since. Shall I give it to you?” And before Riku could refuse — he would have refused, he told himself after, for years he told himself — the Prompter breathed the line, and it was Nagi's voice, and the line was: you were supposed to follow me.

The applause began. Soft, patient, entirely around him, snow settling on a roof at the end of the world. On the stage the performance had changed, and the figure reaching upward wore his own coat, the good one, thread of luck in the hem. Walk out mid-performance, the ferryman had said. It is the only exit they honour. Riku looked at the stage, and at the eleventh row, where one seat held a stillness like deep water. He sat down.
