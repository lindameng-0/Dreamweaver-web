He woke in a white room with the door open.

There was no moment of waking. One thing became another the way one field becomes the next field when you have been walking a long time without looking up. Linen over him, cool and heavy. A window, and past the window a column, and past the column the light.

He lay still and listened. Water somewhere, running slow. Birds, several of them, in different places, none of them close. No boots on stone. No voices counting stock. No wind moving through anything that had been broken.

He put his hand on his ribs.

He pressed. Then he pressed harder, with the flat of two fingers, down into the place under the bone where the ache had lived for eleven days, and there was nothing there. He pressed until his knuckles whitened. He drew a breath as deep as his lungs would take and let it out slowly and drew another, and the second one went further than the first.

He stayed like that for a while.

The room had a table in it. On the table there was bread under a cloth, and beside the bread a shallow dish of water with three petals floating in it, blue, the yellow very small at their centers. Nobody had knocked. Nobody had come in. He looked at the dish for a long time and then he ate the bread standing up, the way he had eaten everything for eight years, and went out through the door.

The terrace ran east and west and had no railing.

He stopped at the edge of it. Below him the land fell away in steps of white stone, each one carrying its own weight of color, archway beneath archway, and the color ran downhill in long drifts the way water finds the low ground, pooling scarlet where the terraces sank, thinning to white and gold where they climbed. Vines came up over the walls and out along the tops of the columns and hung there in a slow spill. Far down, the valley floor went green and level, and the water crossed it without hurry, and white houses stood among the flowers with their doors open.

A bird went past beneath his feet.

He watched it cross from one terrace to the next and settle somewhere he could not see, and then he looked up, because the sky had done something. The overcast was gone. There had been cloud over Auralis every day of his life, a grey lid pulled tight from one edge of the world to the other, and he had stopped seeing it the way you stop seeing your own hands. Above him now there was blue, and depth in the blue, and high white shapes moving through it so slowly that he had to pick one and hold his eyes on it to prove that it moved at all.

He stood there until his neck hurt.

"You're up early."

A woman was coming along the terrace with a basket against her hip. She was perhaps thirty, barefoot, with her sleeves pushed past her elbows, and she said it the way you say it to someone you have known for years and expect to keep knowing.

He turned. In the half second before he answered, he took the terrace apart. Two ways off it, the steps behind her and the drop. She was carrying the basket on the side that would slow her. Nobody else within forty feet.

"I am," he said.

"Everyone is, the first week." She shifted the basket. "It wears off. Give it a month and you'll be like the rest of us, out of bed at noon and blaming the light." She nodded at the drop past his shoes. "The lower path's easier on the knees, if you're going down. And the cistern steps are slick until the sun gets round to them. Lia went over them in the spring and complained about it for a season."

"I'll take the lower path."

"Sensible." She was already moving. "There's bread in the west arcade if you didn't get any."

"I got some."

"Good," she said, and went on, and did not ask him a single thing.

He listened to her feet on the stone until they were gone.

That was the part he kept coming back to afterward, though he did not put words to it then. Not the sky. Not the flowers. Nobody had asked him where he was from. Nobody had looked at his forearm.

The stairs went down for a long time.

They were shallow and worn in the middle where feet had gone for years, and at every landing the view rearranged itself, more valley, less sky, until the terraces closed overhead and he was walking inside a colonnade with the light coming through in bars. Small flowers had come up in the joints between the stones. He stepped over them without thinking and then noticed that he had, and did not know what to make of it.

Eli was sitting on the bottom step with his back to the climb.

His wings were folded and open along the ground on either side, white, taking up most of the width of the stair, and there was a cat's worth of small brown birds walking on them. One of them was pulling at a feather near the joint with a great deal of commitment. Eli was letting it.

"There you are," he said, without turning around.

Luca stopped four steps up.

"Sit down before they leave. They won't come near anyone standing." Eli tipped his head back until he could see him upside down. He looked absurd doing it. "Slowly."

Luca sat down slowly.

The birds did not leave. One of them came off the wing and onto the step between them and stood there considering his boot, and then went back to what it had been doing.

"They do this every morning?"

"They do this to me every morning. I've never once seen it happen to anybody else." Eli watched the one at the joint work the feather loose. "Sylvia would say something about carrion."

"Sylvia would be right."

"Sylvia is always right. It's her worst habit." The feather came free. The bird went off with it low over the flowers, and the rest went after, and the light came back to the stair all at once where the wings had been shadowing it. Eli folded them in and stood up and put out his hand, and Luca looked at the hand.

Then he took it, and was pulled to his feet, and Eli let go at exactly the moment a person lets go.

They walked.

The colonnade ran west along the hill and gave out onto a walk of pale gravel that went down through the orchards, and the orchards were in flower, and the flowers came off the branches in the wind and got into everything. Eli named things as they passed them. That cistern is older than the wall. That path goes nowhere, I like it anyway. Those are Serrin's bees and he will talk to you about them for an hour if you let him, so don't let him. He did not explain the Garden. He talked about it the way people talk about a street they grew up on.

At the bottom the water came out from under an arch and widened and went slow, and there were steps down into it, worn round at the edges, and the reflection of the arch sat on the surface without breaking.

Eli sat down on the second step and put his feet in.

"It's cold for about four seconds," he said. "Then it isn't."

Luca stood on the bank with his hands at his sides.

The wind came up the valley. It went across the surface and the reflection of the arch broke into a hundred pieces and every piece was bright.

Luca sat down on the second step and put his feet in.

It was cold for about four seconds.

The days did not arrive in any order he could keep.

That was the thing about them. He had spent his life being able to say where he had been on a given morning and what it had cost, and here a week went by and came apart in his hands, and what was left was a heat on the back of his neck and the sound of somebody laughing two terraces down and the taste of water out of a stone cup.

On one of them they planted the eastern slope.

It had been bare when he first saw it, or nearly, a long shoulder of ground below the unfinished pavilion where the color thinned out and the earth showed through in patches, and Eli had looked at it three days running the way a man looks at a debt. On the fourth morning there were forty people on it with baskets.

"You've done this before," Mina said.

Luca was on his knees in the dirt with his sleeves rolled. "No."

"You've done something." She dropped another handful of seedlings beside his hand and crouched to look at his row, which was straight, and evenly spaced, and firmed down at the base of each stem with two fingers, and went on being straight for eleven feet. She looked back up the slope at her own row, which did not. "That's obscene. Nobody's first row looks like that."

"It's a hole and a plant."

"Listen to him. A hole and a plant." She wiped her forehead with her wrist and left dirt on it. "Eli, come and look at what your friend is doing to me."

Eli came down the slope with a basket on each arm and half the orchard in his hair.

He looked at Luca's row. He looked at Mina's row. He set the baskets down.

"Mina," he said carefully, "what is happening at the end there."

"It's a curve."

"It's a question mark."

"It's a curve, and it's deliberate, and in three weeks when it comes up you'll all be saying how the eastern slope has such a natural feel to it." She threw a clod at him. It went wide. "Not everything has to be in lines."

"Everything has to be in lines," Eli said, with such immediate and total sincerity that Mina put her face in her hands and Luca, kneeling in the dirt with a seedling in his fist, felt his mouth do something it had not done in years and turned his head away before anybody saw it.

The boy who worked the cistern came down with water. Lia came with more baskets and stayed to argue about the spacing and lost. Somebody had brought a flute and was not good at it. The sun came round over the pavilion and the shadows of the broken columns went across the slope one after another, and the row grew, and Luca's hands were black to the wrist, and he did not think about a single thing for four hours.

When they got to the last of the ground Eli knelt down beside him with the final basket between them.

"Whatever you like," he said. "It's your end of the row."

Luca went through the basket.

He knew what he was doing while he did it. That was the trouble with him and always had been. He took his time about it, turned over the white ones and the tall gold ones and a red thing with a heavy head, and he took out five small blue ones with the yellow at their centers so small it was barely there, and he put them in the ground at the end of his row, and firmed them down with two fingers, and said nothing at all.

Eli did not say anything either.

But when Luca looked up, he had stopped moving entirely, and the wing on that side had come half open the way a door comes open in a house with a draft, and he was looking at five blue flowers in the dirt as though somebody had put a hand through his chest.

Then the rain came over the ridge and everyone ran.

It came fast and warm and heavy, and forty people went up the slope shouting with baskets over their heads, and the pavilion took them all. Water came off the unfinished edge of the roof in a sheet and stood in the air as spray and the whole slope below went dark and shining. Somebody had saved the bread. Somebody had not saved the flute. Mina sat with her back against a column and her legs out and pronounced the eastern slope finished, curve and all, and nobody argued because it was raining and there was bread.

Luca sat with his back against the column beside her.

He noticed that he had done it. He noticed it the way you notice a coin in a pocket you have not worn since winter, and he stayed there, and let the stone take his weight, and watched the water come off the roof.

Eli stood at the open side with the rain going past a hand's width from his face.

Behind him someone had found the flute. It came up out of the mud in two pieces and was presented to the pavilion like evidence at a trial, and the boy who had been playing it took it in both hands and made a sound of such genuine grief that Mina laughed until she had to put her head between her knees.

"Serrin will make you another one."

"Serrin makes them out of reed."

"What was this one made of?"

"Reed," the boy admitted.

The rain came harder. Water went off the broken edge of the roof in a sheet and stood in the air where it landed, and the smell that came up off the turned ground reached them all at once, warm and black and green, and for a while nobody said anything, because there was nothing worth saying over it.

Lia started the song.

It was not a good song and she did not have a good voice for it, and by the second line there were six of them, and by the third the boy with the two pieces of flute was beating time on a column with the heel of his hand and had forgotten about the flute entirely. Mina sang the low part badly and on purpose. Someone at the back had the words wrong and would not be corrected. The rain kept on. Luca sat against his column with his knees up and his hands filthy and did not sing, and at the turn of the verse Mina reached over without looking and thumped him twice on the arm, in time, the way you do to somebody who is with you.

He did not move away.

Then the rain stopped the way it does, all at once, and the sun came in under the edge of the cloud and along the whole eastern slope at the height of a man's shoulder, and every wet thing on that hillside caught fire.

Forty people went out of the pavilion at once.

The ground steamed. It came up off the new rows in long ropes that leaned east with the light coming through them, and the water standing in the joints of the broken pavement threw the sky back up in pieces, and the flowers on the terraces above were so heavy with rain that they had all bowed their heads in the same direction, mile after mile of them, as though something had passed and they had watched it go.

The shouting started behind him.

The boy with the two pieces of flute had got hold of one of the low vines coming off the pavilion wall, the whole length of it loaded with rain, and he brought it down over Lia's head with the timing of a person who had done it before and intended to keep doing it. Lia went from dry to not dry between one word and the next. What she said about it went up and down the slope and came back off the terraces above them, and Mina had to sit down again.

Luca had already stepped out of range.

He did it without hurrying and without appearing to have moved, the way he came off a doorway, and he stood four feet clear with his arms folded and watched the boy work his way along the wall through the rest of them.

Mina was laughing so hard she had gone quiet.

The boy came past him with the vine up. He looked at Luca. Luca looked back, unbothered, filthy to the wrist, entirely still, and something in the boy's face reconsidered the whole enterprise, and he went by.

Luca let him go by.

Then he reached up, put one hand flat against the underside of the pavilion's broken gutter where the whole roof had been draining into a hollow in the stone since the rain began, and pushed.

It went over the boy from the shoulders down in one weight.

There was a silence of about a second and a half.

Then the eastern slope came apart. Lia was on her knees. Mina had a hand out against a column and could not straighten. The boy stood in the wreckage of his own campaign with his hair flat to his skull and both halves of the flute still in his fist and turned around very slowly to look at the man who had not moved at all, who had not so much as unfolded his arms, who was watching him with an expression of mild and total indifference.

"That," the boy said, "was a lot of water."

"It was," Luca agreed.

Eli watched it happen from the pavement, and did not laugh, and did not say anything. He stood very still with his hands loose at his sides and the wings gone slack behind him, looking at Luca the way a man looks at weather coming in over a ridge he has been watching all his life.

The next morning the boy brought him a reed.

The days went on being like that.

He learned where the cistern steps were slick and stopped taking them anyway. He learned that Serrin's bees were in the old amphitheatre, in the gap under the seventh tier where the stone had shifted, and that Serrin would in fact talk about them for an hour and that the hour was not unpleasant. He learned that Mina got to the west arcade before the light did, and that if you were there at that hour you were fed first and expected to carry something. He learned twenty names and then thirty, and then stopped counting, which was a thing he had never once done with a name in his life.

A woman on the lower path went past him with a jar on her shoulder and the strap gave out. He caught the jar. He did not catch it and hand it to her and receive her thanks, because by the time she had turned around he had set it upright on the wall and was already walking, and she called after him and he lifted a hand without looking back.

At the amphitheatre in the afternoons the acoustics did what they had been built to do. A child stood at the center of the floor and said one word and the word came back off the tiers to everyone at once, and then thirty people wanted a turn, and the whole bowl of white stone filled up with nonsense for two hours.

He sat on the ninth tier and let it happen.

On the eleventh day somebody shouted his name across two terraces to ask whether he was coming down, and he answered before he had decided to.

He thought about that one for a while afterward.

On the fourteenth night the two of them went up to the pavilion, because the eastern slope had come up.

It had come up thin and pale and low, an inch of it, nothing anyone would call a color yet, and Eli had gone down the rows in the last of the light on his knees like a man reading. Now the light was gone and they were lying on their backs on the pavement between two broken columns, and the stone under them was still giving back the heat of the day.

The sky was full.

It went from one edge of the world to the other and it had depth in it, near ones and far ones and behind those a kind of bright dust that resolved into more the longer he looked, and the whole of it turned very slowly around a point above the northern ridge.

Eli's hand came up.

It went up the way it had gone up on a wet roof under a grey lid, three fingers loose, the wrist ready, and the gold came to the tips of them and went out over the dark. It found a star and stopped. It went to the next one and stopped. It laid itself down between the two of them and stayed there, thin and warm and steady against all that cold white, and then it went on to a third.

The harp came out of the sky first.

Then the square, and the bright one at its corner. Then the long trailing one with the hook at the end that he had watched arrive over a city and had thought at the time was invented, and it was there, it was all there, every angle of it, the hook bending east exactly as far as it had bent on the underside of the cloud.

Forty-one shapes, and not one of them out by a degree.

Luca watched the gold arrive at the crooked seven leaning east and go down its length, one, two, three, and touch the dim one at the bend, and put the thread through it and carry on.

Eli lowered his arm and the whole sky stayed lit, gold laid over white from one horizon to the other, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"Say it doesn't work," Luca kept his eyes on the sky.

"Say what doesn't work."

"The edge. Going out past it." He did not turn his head. "Say you get out there and it's more and more barren land that won’t yield, people that no longer exist."

The gold burned quietly above them.

"You'll come down through the valley," Eli said. "Over the water and up the other side. And the first house you come to, the door will be open, because they'll have heard you coming and they won't have anything to shut it against."

"That isn't the question."

"It's the answer to it."

Luca turned his head then, and looked at him, and Eli was still watching his own work with his hands lying on the warm stone and his face perfectly clear, and there was nothing in it that was pretending. That was the part. He had spent eleven years learning to find the place where a person’s certainty went soft and he could not find it here, and he had looked for two weeks.

"You've never once said if," Luca said.

"No."

"Not one time."

"No," Eli said again, and then he turned his head as well, and they were looking at each other with a sky full of gold above them, and he smiled, slow and unhurried and enormously kind. "Neither have you."

The wind came up the slope and went over the new rows and past them and out.

Luca looked back at the sky and did not answer, and his thumb moved once against his forefinger, and above him the shapes stood exactly where a man on a roof had put them a long way from here, under a ceiling of cloud, with no way on earth of knowing.

"Say it doesn't work," Luca said.

"Say what doesn't work."

"The edge. Going out past it." He did not turn his head. "Say you get out there and it's ground and it's grey and there's nothing in it that answers you."

The gold burned quietly above them.

"They'll come out to the road," Eli said. "It won't be all of them at first. It'll be one, and then the ones who were watching that one."

"I said if."

"I know what you said."

"Then answer it."

Eli turned his head on the stone.

"I am," he said.

He was smiling. It was small and it went all the way, and there was nothing behind it doing anything else.

"You want me to tell you what I'd do standing somewhere I'm not going to be standing," Eli said. "I can't. I've never been able to. People ask me. And I open my mouth and there's nothing there to say, because when I look ahead I see the thing, Luca. I don't see the ways it doesn't happen."

Luca sat up. He did it before he had decided to, and then he was sitting with his forearms on his knees and the whole lit sky in front of him and Eli lying flat on the stone beside him, and the words were already out.

"When you look at it," he said, "how much of it do you get?"

"All of it. The world like this. Exactly like this. That's the end of it," Eli said. "That's how it ends."

"And the rest."

"There isn't a rest."

Luca did not say anything. He had been four years old the first time. He remembered the room and he remembered the light in it and he remembered that it had not sounded like a guess.

Above them the shapes went on standing where a man on a roof had put them, under a ceiling of cloud, with no way on earth of knowing.

"You're going to be wrong about something one day," Luca said.

"Probably."

"You won't survive it."

Eli laughed. It came out quiet and genuinely delighted, and he put his hands behind his head and did not take his eyes off his own gold, and said, "Then you'll be there."

The wind came up the slope and went over the new rows and past them and out.

Luca lay back down.

He put his hands on his stomach and his thumb moved once against his forefinger and he looked up into forty-one shapes he could have named with his eyes shut, and he did not say anything more.

There was a noise the Garden made in the mornings that he had stopped hearing by the fourth day. Water in the channels, birds, somebody's door on its hinge, the whole of it so even that he had gotten accustomed to ignoring it. This time he woke up.

He lay there and did not move. He thought, for a moment, that it was singing.

Something occurred to him, distantly, the way things do before you are properly awake. Then a man's voice came up over the top of the rest and cracked in the middle of a word, and Luca was out of bed with his hand on the door and no memory at all of the space between.

He opened it.

Fifty people. No, more, and though they were standing in a crowd, not one of them were facing the same direction. At the back, a woman sat on the fountain stones with her arms around her knees and somebody's coat across her shoulders that did not fit her. A man walked the whole width of the courtyard carrying nothing, going nowhere, and stopped in the middle and stayed there.

The light was beautiful. It came down through the vines on the arch and lay in pieces on the stone. Every door on the near terrace stood open, the way every door in this place always did.

Eli was at the front of them with his back to Luca's door. He had been talking for hours. Luca could hear it in the shape of the sentences, the way a man's speech goes when he has said the same thing five times and has begun to arrange it better each time.

"They are not out there."

"Eli—"

"If the Citadel had found this place we would not be standing here talking about it. They do not send one person in the dark to shoot somebody walking near the edge and then go home. That is not how they have ever done anything and every one of you has seen how they do it. They come in daylight. They come in numbers. They burn what they cannot carry."

"Then how—"

"And they would need a road to come down."

He raised his voice a little for that, not much, only enough to carry to the back of the crowd.

"There is no road. There is no gate. There is no direction any of you could point in with a knife at your throat. I brought every person here myself, one at a time, and I have never told the way to another living soul and I will not while I am breathing. That is the only door there is. There is no second one. Do you trust me?"

Somebody said his name. He went on over it.

"That's a gunshot wound, Eli."

"I know what it is."

"Only Citadel has guns."

"A rifle is a thing. It's an object. Things get carried and traded and dug out of the ground and turn up two hundred miles from where anyone made them.” He said it lightly, almost as an aside, “It does not mean what you are all deciding it means."

"Then what does it mean."

"It means we don't know yet."

He let that sit for a moment, let the arguing subside, before he went on.

That was the first thing Luca understood, standing in his doorway with nobody aware of him yet. A man near the front had his arms folded and had already let his shoulders down, and the woman beside him was nodding in the small unconscious way people nod when they have decided something a moment before they know it.

Eli kept going.

"Corin was found at the far side. Something happened out there before it was light and none of us understand it yet and we will. In the meantime I would rather all of you ate something and sat down, because you have been standing in this courtyard since dark and it is not helping him. Just let me deal with this."

*Corin.*

That was the second thing, and it came slowly, because the name meant almost nothing to him. Luca had spoken to the man twice, both times about nothing at all, and what came back to him now was a passerby going down the steps under the arches early in the day, and a hand on the stone at the turn.

Nobody in that courtyard had needed telling what the wound was.

There were people standing on that pale stone in the morning light with the flowers coming out of the seams under their feet who had opened men from the collarbone down, and had done it close, and had gone on afterward to open the next one. They had known what it was since before dawn, the way you know your own name. They had only wanted somebody to say the word, because as long as nobody said it, it was still possible that it was something else.

It was not something else.

"Somebody had one," a man said.

He was to the left of the fountain. The blood had come down the front of him from the collar and dried in a shape that had run. He had not washed and had not changed and by the look of him had not sat down.

"That's what I said."

"Somebody had one." He turned it over out loud, slowly, "Where."

"Jay."

"Where did somebody have one." Jay looked up. "You have been telling us all morning that nobody can get in here. I believe you. I do believe you."

Eli said nothing.

"So where was it."

The courtyard went quiet in a way it had not been quiet all morning.

"Here," Mason said.

He said it very quietly and it went the whole width of the place anyway.

"No." someone whispered.

"It was here." His face had changed before his mouth did and he sounded almost sorry about it. "It came in the way we came in. In somebody's hands, at some point, because that is the only way anything gets here at all, and he has stood there for four hours telling us so."

Somebody further back said no again.

"It has been in a room in this place. It has been in one of these houses." He turned around to all of them. "It was in one this morning."

"No." This time it was Eli.

"Tell me another way. Any of you. Give me another way and I will take it and I will be glad of it."

Nobody gave him another way.

There were about four seconds in which the only sound was water. The light came down through the vines on the arch and lay in pieces on the stone. Forty open doors on the near terrace, and fifty people standing among them, not looking at each other.

"Enough," Eli said. "You are frightening people with a story."

"Am I wrong."

"You are frightening them."

"Am I wrong. Say it and I'll sit down and I won't open my mouth again today."

Eli's mouth was open and nothing came out of it.

A second and a half, perhaps. Luca had never seen it. Not in a room, not on a wall, not with a man bleeding out on the floor between them. The hand still half raised. The words not arriving.

Behind the fountain, a door shut.

It went through the courtyard and left a mark. There were no locks in the Garden. No bolts, no bars, nothing that closed, nothing that had ever needed to, and fifty people turned toward the sound of a door being pulled to and stood there looking at it.

The woman on the fountain stones got up and went to the arch and stopped, because there was nowhere to go, and put her back against the stone.

Then they started counting. Nobody announced it. A woman near the arch went along a row touching shoulders and saying names under her breath, and inside a minute four of them were doing it in different corners of the courtyard, and someone shouted a name across the whole width of the place and someone shouted back that he was down at the water. He had been at the water since dawn. He was fine.

"Three years," Mason said.

He had not spoken all morning. He said it from over by the arch, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man going slowly because he wanted very much to be wrong.

"Three years this place has stood. Not a fight. Not a theft." He was looking at the ground. "And this morning."

Nobody answered him.

"So what is different about this morning."

It was not cruel. It was not aimed at anything. It went down the courtyard the way a stone goes down a slope, and by the time it reached the far wall it had turned into something else entirely.

"Who came here last," somebody said.

Fifty people turned toward the door. Eli turned with them.

Luca was standing in it.

Nobody said anything.

They were not baying for it. That was the part he would keep. Fifty people, most of whom had been decent to him for two weeks, standing very still and waiting to hear the answer to a reasonable question, and not one of them wanting it to be him.

They only wanted it to be someone.

"Eli," he said.

"No."

"Eli." He put his hand on Eli's arm. Eli turned, and his expression was strangely shuttered. "It's all right. Let go."

He looked past him, at the crowd.

They had come in a little without anyone deciding to. The gap that had been there when he opened the door was gone and the front of it stood close enough that he could see the dirt under a woman's nails and the wet on Jay's forearms where the blood had not gone all the way dry. Somebody at the back moved and the whole of it moved with him, a slow shift like water finding the low side of a bowl.

Fifty faces. He knew about twenty of them. Every one of them was already looking at him and had been for some time.

"Ask me," he said.

Jay did not ask him anything.

He stood there with his hands hanging and looked at Luca the way you look at a word you have read four times and still cannot make sense of, and when he finally spoke it came out too quiet for the back of the courtyard.

"Where are you from."

"Under the ring."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somebody near the arch shifted their weight and the sound of it carried.

"Under it," Jay said.

"Yes."

"The sewers."

"Yes."

"Say the rest of it, then." He had gone very still. "Because I have heard a lot of men say they were from underneath. I heard it every other week in the mid ring, out of people I could see the wall from where they slept. It is an easy thing to say and there is nobody left to argue with it."

"They died fast down there," said a man behind him. "That's what I always heard. Faster than anywhere."

"They did," Luca said.

"So how many are actually left."

"I don't know. Not much now, by the looks of it. Nobody bothers to look for anyone else, and that’s for the best." He said it the way you answer a question about the weather in a place you no longer live. "Even if they die fast, new ones always arrive. Why they came is anybody's guess. Gambled, mugged… regardless, there were always more who lost it all. There were always going to be more."

He said it without heat and it went further across the courtyard than it should have.

"There's a great deal down there. It's only that nobody cares for a place with negative value." He looked at the man who had spoken. "The Citadel does not have a word for us. Not a bad one. They have a word for the rings and they have a word for the green belt and for us they have nothing at all, because they do not name a thing they see as dead."

"Before what."

"Before the burning."

The word landed and Luca watched it land, one row at a time, the way a wind goes across standing grass.

"That was you lot," somebody said, from off to the side. It came out half question.

"That was the collectors."

"Same thing."

"It isn't," Luca said. "You could stand in the sewers your whole life and never be one. Most did. But they came up out of down there, all of them, and there's a reason for that, and if you sit with it for a minute you'll get to it yourself."

Nobody sat with it.

"Say it, then."

"They were the only ones with nothing to lose by it." He let that sit. "It came out of the Citadel and it landed in the green belt and it stopped there and it never moved again, and then the bill got written against the whole slum, evenly, as though we had all had a share of it. That is the part people forget. Evenly." He turned his hand over. "Nobody underneath ever saw a coin of it. Nobody underneath was ever asked. And when the villages in the belt went back for a second loan, to put the next round of it on somebody further out, it was the collectors who went up and took the villages apart, and they did it because they were the only people in the slums for whom the debt was worth precisely nothing. You cannot threaten a person with a bill when they were never going to pay it and everyone already knows they were never going to pay it."

"They killed people."

"Yes."

"A great many people."

"Yes," Luca said. "And then they burned the money, and after that there was no more money, and after that nobody in the slums has been able to buy anything from anyone. Which is the thing you are all standing here trying to work out and none of you wants to say."

Jay said, "Say it."

"You want to know what I'd take."

Nobody answered him.

"That's the question. It's the only question anyone has ever asked out there and it's the one you're asking now." Luca looked at the front row of them. "There's no way into the Citadel. There never was. They are not selling entry and they never were, so nobody has ever gone over for a place inside. They go over for water. They go over for a course of something for a child who is going to die anyway. They go over for a promise that a ring will be left alone one winter, and then the winter comes and they come through it regardless."

"That's true," somebody said quietly, to somebody else.

"That works on a person once. It works best on someone who still believes a thing said out loud has to happen." He shrugged very slightly. "You lose that at different ages depending where you were born. On the green belt you can keep it your whole life. In the rings you lose it somewhere in your twenties. In the sewers you never have it at all, and that's why you've all been standing out here for four hours looking for someone from up close."

"So you're telling us it can't be you."

"I'm telling you what you already think," Luca said. "That’s the test, isn’t it? Who has suffered enough to despise them more."

The courtyard was very quiet.

"That doesn't clear him," Jay said.

He said it to the crowd rather than to Luca, and there was something wrong with his voice.

"I want to be honest about that. It doesn't clear him. He can stand there and say all of it right and a person can be from anywhere and still—" he realized how loud his voice was getting. "You can learn a place. You can sit in a room somewhere with a page in front of you and learn a place well enough to stand in a crowd and present it. That's a thing that happens. Everyone here knows that's a thing that happens."

"Yes."

"Stop doing that."

"You're not wrong."

"Where were you the night before last."

"Asleep."

"Anyone see you go in."

"No."

"Anyone at all."

"No," Luca said. "Nobody saw me."

Jay nodded, several times, slowly, the way a person does when they have been handed the exact answer they were afraid of.

"You walk out to the edge," he said.

"Yes."

"On your own. Every morning. Past the last of the colonnades where there's nobody at all, and back before the sun's over the terraces." His voice was going up and he was not doing it on purpose. "That is the ground he was lying on. That is the exact ground. And not one person here has ever once asked you what you go out there for."

"Nobody's asked."

"Then I'm asking."

Luca said, "I like it out there."

"You like it out there."

"Yes. it’s quiet."

"That's what I get." He came forward and there was nothing violent in it yet, and behind him people were moving in without knowing they were doing it. "Give me one thing. One. Anything I can put my hand on, because I have had someone die here of all places, and because of that it will never be—"

He took Luca by the forearm.

He did not do it to hurt him. He crossed the last of the space ou of impulse, the way a person crosses a room to take something out of another’'s hands before he drops it, and his fingers closed on the sleeve, and the sleeve went up.

Nobody said anything.

Jay's hand was still on him, and he was looking down at his own hand and at the arm under it, and his face had not caught up yet, and Luca stood there and let him look, because there was nothing else to do at that point and there never had been.

Then Jay let go.

He got his fingers off it the way you take your hand off a stove, and went back a step, and then another, and the second was not a step so much as his weight going out from under him and finding somewhere else to be.

The man behind him moved back too. He had not seen anything. He had seen Jay's face and that was enough, and the woman beside him went with him, and it went outward through the front of the crowd in a slow silent shift, two feet, three feet.

The courtyard opened.

Fifty people, and inside four seconds there was nobody within reach of Luca on any side, and half of them could not have told you what they were looking at.

He did not cover it.

He had felt the sleeve go and had known exactly what was happening and had thought, quite clearly, *pull it down*, and had not. The light out there was very good. It came off the pale stone and up into everything and there was nowhere in that place to put an arm where it did not reach.

It was an old thing. It had gone white a long time ago, running the length of his forearm. A scar, the remnants of a clean slit, where blood once ran down. Above it his hand hung open, though he was wearing a black glove, everybody in that courtyard was old enough to have been out on the rings fifteen years ago knew exactly what colour it used to be.

The fabric came down over his wrist. It made almost no sound and it was already far too late.

Luca looked up and found Eli looking at him.

He had turned when the crowd moved. He was standing about six feet off with his weight slightly back and one hand half lifted, stopped where it was, and his face was doing something Luca could not read and did not like.

"You were one of them."

He said it quietly. It was not for the courtyard. It was not a question either, and it did not come out the way anything Eli said usually came out.

Luca did not answer him.

Something went through Eli's face and he put it away. It took about a second and a half.

"Right hand," Jay said, from over by the fountain. He was breathing like someone who had run somewhere. "They wore the one glove on the right hand. Red one. You'd see it coming up the road a long way off before you ever saw the person."

Nobody answered him.

That was the whole of what was said about it. Fifty people and one of them talking, and when he had finished nobody took it up, and nobody put a name to it, and the word that used to go with it was not spoken in that courtyard by anyone, then or afterward.

A woman near the arch had her hand over her mouth and did not take it away.

Two men by the water had come together and one of them was saying something low and fast into the side of the other's head, and the other was not answering and was not looking away.

Three or four had turned their faces off entirely and were looking at the ground, or the stone, or their own hands.

And there was a man on the far side who had not stepped back with the rest and had not stepped forward either. He stood where he was with his arms down and his jaw set and looked at Luca the whole time, and there was nothing frightened in it anywhere.

Mason came out from under the arch.

He was the only person in that courtyard moving toward him instead of away, and he crossed the open ground and stopped four feet off, and he did not look at the sleeve at all.

He looked at the rest of him. The whole of him, top to bottom, the way you look at a thing that does not add up. The wrists. The pale colour of him, devoid of scarring. The shoulders under the shirt, which were not much.

Then he inclined his head, very slightly, the way you do when you have been introduced.

"I'm sorry for what was said to you here," Mason said.

Somebody near the back said that nothing had been said to him that wasn't fair.

"There's a man dead," he said. "Nobody asked him anything I wouldn't answer."

"Nobody asked it of you."

"Ask it, then."

"I'm not asking anybody anything."

"Ask it." He was already coming forward. "I was *inside* one of the blast craters. I'd like to know how many people here can say that, since apparently this is what we're doing now."

And it went.

Luca could not have said afterward how. He was looking at Eli, who had not moved and had not stopped looking at him, and so the whole of the next part arrived sideways, in the way you hear a room you are not in.

They were placing each other.

That was what took him a moment. They were doing it almost gently, the way you do at a table with people you have known for years and never asked, and there was a man near the fountain who kept saying *no, but where, though, how far* to a woman who did not want to answer him and kept laughing and saying it did not matter.

She was from the second ring.

Luca watched it land on the people nearest her. Nothing happened to their faces. Something happened to the space around her, four or five people adjusting where they stood by a few inches, and the man asking did not ask her anything else.

"That's not—" she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You've all got a look."

"Nobody's got a look."

Somebody further off said, to nobody, that the second ring got the water first. Not accusingly. As a fact, the way you'd mention the weather, and then somebody else said that was the third ring and not the second, and then they were arguing about the pipe order, which of the rings had been on it and in what year, and Luca stood there and listened to fifty people who had not thought about the Citadel in three years sort themselves by their distance from it.

They still had the order by heart. All of them. Nobody had to be reminded of anything.

It was only backwards now.

Somebody laughed at something and it came out wrong. Somebody said *fine, you were cold, we were all cold* and somebody said *don't*, quite sharply, and the second voice was a woman Luca had seen every morning for carrying things down the terrace steps, and he had never heard her make a sound like that.

"Don't say we were all cold."

"Ivy—"

"Say it about yourself. Say it about your own." She was not shouting. That was the thing that made people turn. "We were all cold. We were not all cold."

Nobody answered her.

"Eleven of us in one wall in the ninth. Do you know what that is? Eleven bodies against one wall out of the wind and you sleep in the middle if you're small enough and you sleep on the outside if you aren't, and in the morning you find out." She had her hands out in front of her and she was looking at them, not at anybody. "You find out in the morning. That's how you find out."

Somebody said her name again.

"Nine years I have not thought about that," she said. "Nine years."

And then she stopped, and looked around herself, at the water and the arches and the open doors, the way a person does when they wake somewhere unfamiliar.

Luca watched her do it.

He would think about that afterward. It was the way she looked around the courtyard, like a woman coming up out of something, checking where she was.

Because that was what was happening. He could not have put it into a sentence. They were not remembering the outside because they wanted to. It was coming back up through them, one after another, and each one it came up through pulled the next, and there was a woman by the wall with her hand over her mouth who had not spoken once and was crying with her eyes open.

Somebody was saying a number and correcting it. He had started at sixteen and gone to fourteen and then said no, twelve, twelve of them out of that season, and there was a silence around him for a moment, and then somebody in the next argument over said something about the green belt and the whole of the near half of the courtyard turned that way, because belt meant close in, and close in meant a person who still thought a promise had to be kept, and they had spent four hours that morning agreeing that such a person was the kind you could buy.

So it did not go away. It came apart and went into all of them.

They ran out of bidding with what had been done to them and started bidding with what they had done, and then with what they had not done, and then with what other people had failed to do, and by then there were eight or nine of these going at once and none of them were kind and none of them could be stopped by anybody, because everyone who tried to stop one was immediately asked where they were from.

It was that the noise had no way out of itself. Every hand that went up to end it got pulled in, and it kept widening, and nobody in that place had ever done this before and so nobody knew how it was supposed to finish.

A person said the belt word to another, quietly, and the second man's face went and he did not answer.

Somebody brought up a winter nine years ago and something that had been done in it, and said it to a friend, in a completely normal voice, and said it accurately.

A woman put her hand out toward somebody she had lived beside since this place began, and the somebody stepped back out of reach of it.

The light was coming down through the vines on the arch the way it did. There were flowers standing out of the seams in the stone by the fountain, small and white, and the doors on the near terrace were open, and the water went on running in the channel, and Luca found that he was listening for it and had been for some time.

Somebody used the phrase.

He did not catch who. It came out of the middle of an argument about a bad year and it arrived worn smooth, the way a saying does when nobody has thought about it in a long time, and it went by without anyone marking it at all.

Then a man said the one who burned the money had said it first.

And somebody said no. Nobody said it. There was nothing left to count with after that year so people counted with what they had, and it was not declared by anyone, it was only what happened.

They went at that for a while.

Luca stood in the middle of the courtyard with his sleeve down over his wrist and listened to both of them and did not say anything at all.

Behind him a voice went up over the rest and there was a scuffle of feet and somebody said let go of him.

Eli had not turned around.

He was six feet away with his hand still not all the way down and the whole of it happening at his back, and he had not looked at it once. His face had gone somewhere Luca could not follow. Luca had watched him take forty men off a decision that would have killed every one of them, in under a minute, on a road, in the rain, and he had never seen this. What was wrong with it was not the intensity. It was the distance.

Luca opened his mouth.

He got as far as the first sound of a word and stopped and stood there with it in his mouth.

Then somebody in the crowd said that everyone here came out of it, all of us, that was the whole of what this place was, and stopped in the middle of the sentence.

And Luca watched Eli's face come back.

He saw it arrive. Something behind the eyes turning over and catching, the warmth coming up out of wherever he kept it, all of it in less than a second, and then Eli was present and looking outward and reaching for the room the way he always did.

He turned around and walked into it.

He had not heard any of it. He did not know what had been said in the last twenty minutes or who had said it, and he was going to stand in front of fifty people who had just taken each other apart and tell them what this place was for.

Luca did not say anything.

It was a woman near the fountain who said it. She said it to end something, the way you say a thing everybody already agrees with so the room can put itself down, and she got most of the way through before she heard herself.

Everyone here came out of it. All of us. That was what this place was.

She stopped.

She did not finish it and she did not take it back, and by the time she had done neither it was already going along the front of the courtyard the way a crack goes through ice.

Heads turned.

Sable was at the back by the wall with her hands in her sleeves. She had been there all morning. Luca had not thought about her once.

"Where are you from?" someone asked. It did not matter who.

"The green belt," Sable said.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Which one."

"Ashe Row."

Somebody behind Jay said there was an Ashe Row. Not helpfully. Only that there was.

"All your life?"

"Yes."

"Both sides of you as well."

"My mother's people were Ashe Row. My father came from the next one along." She had her hands wrapped in her sleeves and she was speaking to a point on the stone six feet in front of her. "I know what that is. You don't have to say it in a particular way. I know what it is."

"I didn't say anything."

"No." Quietly. "I know."

"So you planted."

"We planted."

"For them."

"For them," she said. "And ate what they let us keep, and it was more than the rings got. I’m sorry."

Nobody had an answer for that straight away.

She was doing well.

Luca stood in the open space they had left around him and watched her do it and could not work out how she was managing it, because she was frightened the whole way through, visibly, in her hands and in the way she had to start sentences twice, and she was still answering every question in the order it came and had not once said anything that went further than she could carry. She had given them the worst thing about herself before they could take it off her.

"You've never been out on the flats, then," somebody said.

"No."

"What year did the north side go."

She said, "I don't know."

Somebody made a noise.

"I don't know." She had not raised her voice. She had not looked up either. "I could tell you something. I could tell you a year and half of you would nod at it and the other half wouldn't and then we'd be doing that instead of this. I'm sorry. I did try to keep it, at the start. And then it was too much and I let it go, and I've never been able to remember it exactly since then."

He could have said something then.

The gap was there and it was wide enough. He was standing in the middle of fifty people with his sleeve down over his wrist, and that morning, in that courtyard, he had been given a kind of standing that not one of them could touch, and it had cost him nothing to get and he was not using it for anything.

He was thinking about the water in the channel and how far off it sounded.

Then the cat came out of her doorway.

It came out the way they do, unhurried, into the light at the top of the terrace steps, and it stopped and put itself down on the warm stone.

Nobody spoke.

There was a sound in that courtyard Luca had never heard in that place, fifty people breathing in at slightly different times, and then nothing at all.

"What is that," somebody said.

Nobody answered. A man was already halfway up the terrace and stopped without getting near it.

"That's an animal."

"That's a ground animal," another man said. He said it flatly, the way you read something aloud.

"There's nothing like that in Auralis."

"There is nothing like that anywhere." Jay's voice was climbing. "There has not been a thing like that on the ground except maybe in the metropolis. There is nothing alive out there. Every single person in this courtyard knows there is nothing alive out there since the Cataclysm—"

The cat got up.

It came down the terrace steps at its own pace with its tail up, past the man who had stopped, and it crossed the open stone in front of fifty people and went to Sable and put the side of its face against her shin and turned and did it again with the other side.

Sable did not move.

"It's mine," she said.

"Where did you get it."

"I've had her a long time—"

"Where did you get it."

And she stopped.

Luca saw her go through it. He saw her take the question and look at what was on the other side and understand that there was nothing she could put there that would not cost her more than the silence would, and he saw her decide.

"Say it," somebody said.

She bent down and picked the cat up.

That was all she did. She got both arms under it and lifted it against her chest and stood there with it, and she did not say anything else for the rest of the morning.

Then it came apart, and it came fast, and it did not stay on the animal more than a few seconds because it did not need to. Twenty people talking at once. Somebody at the back said the word out loud for the first time that day, said *Citadel*, and inside ten seconds it was in every mouth in that courtyard, and somebody was saying that she had been in a village house, that she had stood there and told them so herself, twelve years, she said it herself—

"Her house," a man said. "Go through her house."

"Leave her house—"

"Go through all of them. Every room. Start with hers and do mine after. Do mine first, I don't care, do mine first—"

Eli came up the middle of them.

He did not raise his voice and did not have to. People moved out of his way the way they always had, and he went up onto the terrace steps and put himself between Sable and the rest of it.

"Nobody is going through anybody's house."

"You're from a village."

It came out of the middle of them and it was not raised and it stopped him where he stood.

"Aren't you."

"That has nothing to do with—"

"You planted for them." The man had not come forward. He did not need to. That's the whole of your life. So don't stand up there."

Eli said, "Listen to me."

"No."

That was all. One word, from somebody else entirely, and after it there was nothing in that courtyard for him to talk into.

Eli stood on the steps. He did not move and he did not answer anymore and his face did not do anything at all. He was standing perfectly still with his hands open with nothing more to do.

Somebody went up the steps past him anyway.

That was how it ended. "Don't," Sable said. She had the cat against her chest and had not put it down.

The man in front stopped. She was in the way of the door and he was not, in the end, going to move her out of it.

It swung once from her hand and stopped.

There was a thing set into the inside of it. It was flat and about the size of a thumbnail and it sat under the leather where nothing would rub it, and there was a green light in the face of it.

Nobody said anything. Sable turned the collar over and held it nearer to her face and looked at the thing and then at the place in the leather where it had been sitting, and her mouth was slightly open, and she was doing nothing at all except trying to read it.

It went out and came back.

Luca stood in the open space they had left him and counted it without meaning to. There was a length of time between them and the length of time did not change. It had been doing that in the dark of a house on that terrace every night for however long she had been here, on and off, on and off, patient as a clock, while fifty people slept on the other side of the wall.

"What is this," Sable said.

Nobody answered her.

She looked up. She looked at the man in front of her and then past him at the courtyard, at fifty people who had not made a sound, and Luca watched her go from not knowing to knowing that every other person there did.

"I don't— " she said.

The light went out and came back, and then it came back again before it should have.

Somebody behind Luca said, "It's changed."

"It's changed. Look at it. It's going quicker—"

And Eli moved.

He was on the steps and then he was turned with the bow up and drawn to his cheek, the whole of it in one motion, and every head in the courtyard went with him.

There was a woman standing at the top of the far steps. Eli knew her almost immediately.

Kessler.

She had come in from the direction of the tower. There was blood on her from the shoulder down to the wrist on the left side and it was not dry, and her right hand was flat against the pillar, and there was nothing in either of them.

She opened her mouth.

Eli turned.

His left arm came up and out and the gold came with it, drawn back off the fingers of his right hand along the length of his forearm and gone in the same movement, and the sound of it reached the courtyard afterward, one hard crack of air with nothing else in it.

It took her through the left forearm and carried her off the step.

She hit the tree behind her and the shaft went into the trunk and she went with it, feet off the stone, arm out straight and nailed at the wrist, and her weight came down on the arm and she made a sound then that she did not manage to keep in.

Half the courtyard went backward at once. A woman near the fountain went over the lip of it and nobody picked her up. Somebody was shouting a name.

She got her right hand up before he reached her.

She got it up open and high and empty, as fast as anyone in that courtyard had ever seen a hand go up, and she was still getting it up when he was already there.

He came over the ground in under a second. Not running. Luca saw the wings go and the two strides and then the shadow of them across the whole of the tree, and Eli was standing over her filling the width of the stair, and the light behind him was gone.

His hand came up level with her jaw and a thread went out of it and around her throat and drew.

She did not move after that.

Blood coming down her sleeve to the elbow, arm nailed out to the side, other hand still open above her head. Nothing in her face at all except that she had stopped trying to breathe deeply.

"There was a truce," Eli said.

"There was, and I've kept it. No one has launched an attack since then." She said it flat and fast, because there was a thread on her throat. "I came in alone. I came in like this. You can see my hands."

"Grayson has Citadel's kill switch."

She said it under her breath, close, and nobody past the first step heard a word of it.

The thread drew.

Not much. Enough that her chin went up and stayed there and she stopped talking for a moment, and Luca watched Eli's shoulders and could not tell from them whether the hand had done that on purpose.

"You don't know what that is," she said. "You've no idea what I've just said to you. But all I’m going to say is that he has got hold of something Auralis has never heard of and will not like, and I'm not going to bleed out here explaining it to a desperate mob who won’t take anything that’s not to their liking."

The thread did not move.

"I have soldiers on the north line waiting to hear from me by dark." Her voice was going thin now and she was working around it. "If you do this and you'll be at war with a third of Citadel inside the week over a stretch of dirt, while Grayson does what he likes with the rest of it."

Eli did not answer.

He stood over her without moving, and Luca could not see his face, only the set of his shoulders and the wings and the light going through them, and it went on. Ten seconds. Somebody in the crowd said something and was shushed.

The thread stayed where it was.

Then his hand shifted, and it did not open.

"Not here," Eli said.

Quietly, and it carried the whole width of the courtyard.

"Enough of this place has been bled on this morning. I won't have someone else executed in front of them."

The arrow came out of the tree and out of her arm and was gone, and she came down onto the step and did not have time to fall, because his hand had her by the back of the collar before her knees reached the stone.

Then the air went out of the top of the courtyard.

That was how it always went, and Luca had seen it four times now and it had not once stopped being wrong. A sound like a door pulled shut in a room three floors up. The vines on the arch above the stair went one way and came back. Where the two of them had been there was the tree with a white split in the bark and nothing else, and past it the empty stair, and past that the terraces going down, and every one of the fifty people in that courtyard understood, in the same second, that they were now the only ones in the Garden.
