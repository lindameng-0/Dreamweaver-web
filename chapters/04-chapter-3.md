The guards saw them before they reached the gate. Steel sang as the first one drew his blade, and Eli watched the way the torchlight caught the edge, the way the guard's shoulders set into that familiar stance of suspicion. Harlan. Good instincts. Eli had always liked that about him.

"General." The word was a greeting and a question, and Harlan's eyes did not leave the stranger.

"Harlan." Eli stopped a few paces from the threshold, letting his wings settle, letting the silence do its work. "Long night?"

"Longer now." The blade stayed raised, its edge trained on the young man's chest. "Who's this?"

Eli opened his mouth to answer, but the young man moved first.

It was not a lunge, not an attack, just a single step forward. The guards tensed, fingers tightening on hilts, but all the young man did was raise his arm, turning his wrist so the soft underside faced the torchlight.

Bare skin, unmarked where a Metropolis citizen's pass would have been etched into the flesh.

Eli watched him, curious despite himself. It was an interesting and expected choice. A declaration of what he was not, nothing more, nothing less, offered like a knife by the blade.

"There's no one left out there." The second guard's voice came out strained, his eyes darting between the unmarked wrist and the outside. "Everyone who survived is already here since…"

Harlan couldn’t finish the sentence, but Eli could see the shape of it forming in his mind, suspicion beginning to solidify. A man with no mark and no origin. A ghost from a world that had no space for ghosts.

Eli smiled.

"He's with me."

The words were simple, warm, the kind of thing said between friends over drinks about matters of no consequence. He let his hand drift to his side as he spoke, his fingers brushing the air in a gesture that might have been absent habit.

The tension in Harlan’s shoulders eased by degrees. The question was still there, Eli could see it, hovering just behind his eyes, but it seemed suddenly less urgent, less important, a thing that could wait until morning or perhaps be forgotten entirely in the shuffle of longer nights.

"Of course, General." Harlan lowered his blade. "My apologies."

The gate creaked open. Eli stepped through first, breathing in the familiar air of Auralis, the faint sweetness of night-blooming jasmine from someone's window box, the distant murmur of voices from the residential quarter. The young man followed without a word, his gaze fixed straight ahead as if the guards had already ceased to matter. Perhaps, to him, they never had.

They walked through streets Eli knew by heart, past windows still glowing with the small stubborn lights of those who could not sleep, and the young man did not look at any of it. He moved the way water moves, finding the path of least resistance, and Eli watched and wondered what it would take to make him turn.

The elevator hummed as it climbed.

Eli’s quarters occupied the eastern corner of the floor, far from the offices where the other generals conducted their business. He had chosen it for the light, once, for the way the morning sun came through the windows. But the curtains were drawn now, and the air that met them when he pushed open the door was stale with weeks of absence. Dust had gathered on the books across the desk, on the manuscripts lining the shelves, on teacups he could not remember leaving. The fireplace held a weak flame, and a single lamp fought the dark without much conviction.

"My quarters," Eli said, stepping aside. "It’s warmer than outside, at least."

A figure emerged from somewhere to his left, murmuring *general Eli, you've returned*, and then something else, lower, a reminder that did not need to be heard to be understood. Eli answered with a gesture, two fingers, a tilt of the wrist. *Please. Some privacy.* The man withdrew into one of those dark pockets the lamplight could not reach, and Eli felt the weight of his gaze settle there, patient and unmoving. He would not go far. None of them ever did. Eli turned away from that corner of the room and did not look at it again.

The young man had stopped just inside the threshold. His gaze moved across the room, and Eli could not tell what he saw there, whether he noticed the way the spines of certain books had cracked from overuse or the way others remained untouched, whether he registered the single wilted flower on the windowsill or the cold hearth tools that had not been lifted in weeks. His face gave nothing.

"Your interrogation room is quite distinctive."

“Interrogation?” Eli laughed. He crossed to the side table where a tea set waited, and poured a cup with the ease of someone who had done this a thousand times before, for a thousand different guests, and held it out between them. “I only wanted to offer you tea."

The young man looked at the cup. Then at Eli. He crossed the room in three unhurried strides and lowered himself into the armchair by the fire. One arm along the armrest, one ankle over the opposite knee. He did not take the cup.

"What did you put in it?"

"Honey." Eli smiled. "You don't like sweet things?"

"I don't like being poisoned."

Eli brought the cup to his own lips and drank. The tea was lukewarm, the honey too sweet. He set it on the mantle, “Perhaps another time, then.”

He turned toward the fireplace. Let his shoulders settle. Let the young man watch the back of his head and draw whatever conclusions he liked from the vulnerability of it.

"I apologize in advance," he said, his voice carrying just enough to fill the room and no further. "A small formality. Headquarters regulations." He glanced over his shoulder, "I'm sure you understand."

He raised his hand, and the thread spun out from his fingertips, drifting across the space between them to settle around the young man's wrist.

The young man must’ve felt it, but he did not move. Did not look down.

"And what," he asked, his voice terribly calm, "are you trying to confirm?"

"Your dreams."

The fire crackled. Neither of them moved. Eli watched the young man's face, and finally caught it—a pause in his breathing, half a second, less, before the rhythm resumed.

"I don't dream."

The young man’s words carried a edge, a warning perhaps, but he remained still, watching Eli with the detached interest of someone observing a performance that had already begun to bore him.

Eli's fingers stilled against the mantle. He did not believe it, of course. Everyone dreamed. The mad, the broken, the ones who wished they couldn't. They dreamed in fragments, in static, in the formless dark. But to not dream at all—

"That's a shame," he said, and let out a small sigh. "Dreams are such interesting things."

The young man smiled. It was the first time Eli had seen anything like it on his face, and it did not reach his eyes.

"What are you waiting for?"

Eli opened his mouth to answer, some easy reply already forming, and then he realized. The thread was there, wrapped around the young man's wrist, and yet nothing had happened. Just the room. Just the fire. Just the two of them, exactly where they had been—

And then the world collapsed.

***

Eli opened his eyes and could not remember closing them.

There was nothing here. He understood that immediately, the way one understands cold or hunger, a knowledge that bypassed thought entirely and settled somewhere deeper. Nothing above him, nothing below, nothing in any direction he turned. This had never been anything, never tried to be something, rejected being everything.

Eli did not choose a direction to walk because there were no directions to choose, only the movement of his legs and the absence of everything else. His footsteps made no sound. He was not sure they were footsteps at all.

*Where were the flowers?*

The thought arrived and he let it go. It did not belong here. Not when the very space around him seemed to recoil from his presence, the darkness drawing back and pressing close in the same breath, as if it could not decide whether to swallow him or spit him out. Even if there had been anything here once, he was not sure it would have survived him.

He kept walking.

He did not know how long. Time did not work here the way it worked elsewhere, if it worked at all, if the word meant anything in a place like this. He counted his breaths instead and then forgot to count and then remembered and then forgot again. The nothing did not change. It did not deepen or lighten, did not shift or settle. It simply continued, the same black pressing against him from all sides, filling his mouth when he breathed, sitting on his shoulders like hands. He had walked through so many dreams. He had thought he understood them. He had thought, perhaps, that he had seen everything they could be.

*Where were the flowers?*

And then, between one step and the next, there was something.

Eli stopped. A figure stood ahead of him, or what passed for ahead in a place without direction. It wore a cloak impossibly black, even more so than the space around them. After all this emptiness, after all this nothing that pressed and pressed and gave nothing back, here was a shape that held itself against the dark.

So the young man could dream after all.

It was a small thought, and Eli held it the way one might hold a match in a long night. He had been right. He was no prophet, but he had hoped, and he had been right.

His feet moved before he told them to.

One step, then another, faster now, though he could not have said why the urgency had settled into his bones so suddenly, why his heart had begun to beat in a rhythm he did not recognize, the way a body might respond to falling before the mind caught up. He could not tell if he was getting closer, could not tell if distance meant anything here, but he kept walking, kept moving toward that figure in the dark. He had waited a long time for this. He could wait a little longer. But his feet did not seem to agree.

One more step, and——his hand met glass.

The cold of it shot through his palm and up his arm and settled somewhere behind his ribs. He had not seen the barrier. There was nothing to see, only this invisible wall between himself and the figure on the other side. Eli pressed his palm flat against it and felt nothing give. He pressed harder. In dreams, walls were only walls if you believed in them. He had walked through a thousand barriers with nothing but the certainty that they could be dissolved, because that was what dreams were, in the end. They were belief made manifest. They were will given form.

The glass did not move.

He was close enough now to see how the cloak hung perfectly still, how the shoulders did not rise or fall with breath. The dark hair. The paleness of a neck. He thought of the young man in the armchair by the fire, the way he had smiled before. The same person. It had to be. And Eli understood, with a certainty that settled into him like ice water, that this young man had been here for a very long time. That he had been here before Eli arrived and would be here after Eli left. That he was not trapped in this place.

He was this place.

*Where were the flowers?*

The question rang through the dark, and Eli could not tell anymore if it was his own thought or something else entirely, something that had been waiting here in the nothing, asking and asking and asking, long before he came.

There were no flowers.

The cold had seeped past his wrist now, climbing toward his shoulder, settling into his bones like something that intended to stay. He kept his hand on the glass anyway. He did not know what else to do.

The young man turned.

It was not a dramatic motion. He simply turned, the way anyone might turn when they felt eyes on their back, and then he was facing Eli, and his eyes——

***

Eli woke with his hand still raised, fingers reaching for something that was no longer there.

The room came back to him slowly. The dying fire. The dust. The weight of his own body in a chair he did not remember sitting in. He was cold, colder than he had any right to be, a cold that had followed him back from that place of nothing and settled into the marrow of his bones. His fingers would not stop trembling. He told himself it was the cold.

The young man sat in the armchair across from him, exactly where he had been, head bowed, dark hair fallen across his face. He had not moved. He might have been asleep, might have been dead, might have been a statue placed there by some cruel and patient hand. Eli watched him and did not breathe and could not look away.

He rose from his chair. The floorboards did not creak. The shadows along the walls watched him the way they always watched him, and he crossed the room without feeling his feet touch the ground, because there was only one thing now.

His fingers moved before he meant them to. They found the dark hair and pushed it back, and he did not know why he needed to see, only that the need had already made the decision for him, the way a hand reaches for a door it knows it should not open.

The young man's eyes were already open.

He had been awake. He had been awake the whole time, watching through the curtain of his hair as Eli stumbled back into himself, as Eli crossed the room, as Eli reached for him with hands that would not stop shaking. And now those eyes looked up at him, and Eli understood where the cold had come from.

There was nothing wrong with him. That was the strangest part. His face was a face, his features arranged the way features ought to be arranged, and yet looking at him felt like looking at a door that opened onto nothing. Just a face.

But his eyes.

These eyes held nothing.

And Eli looked into them and felt, for the first time in a very long time, that he was standing at the edge of something that did not have an edge, that he was looking into a darkness that would swallow whatever he sent into it and never give it back. He thought of black holes. He thought of the stories they told about people who stared too long into the spaces between stars and forgot how to look at anything else. He thought, and then he stopped thinking, because thinking felt dangerous here.

"Are you satisfied?"

The young man's voice was soft. Soft and cold and utterly without warmth, and his lips barely moved when he spoke.

Eli did not answer. He could not. His hand was still there, frozen at the young man's temple, and he could not remember how to pull it back.

Then he laughed.

It came out warm and easy, only a little unsteady at the edges, and he let his hand fall, let the familiar looseness return to his posture as if he had simply misplaced it for a moment and found it again. "My apologies," he said, "It seems I found the wrong person."

The young man did not blink. "I can go, then?"

"Of course." Eli stepped aside, swept his arm toward the door in a gesture that was graceful and unhurried and meant absolutely nothing. "The door isn't locked."

The silence stretched between them, thickening with each second that passed, and neither of them moved toward the door. The fire had burned down to embers. The shadows pressed closer. Eli kept his smile in place and waited, because he knew, and the young man knew, and they both knew that the other knew.

The young man smiled. It did not reach his eyes. Nothing reached his eyes.

He rose from the chair in one fluid motion, turned his back to Eli, and walked to the bookshelf along the far wall. His fingers trailed across the spines, disturbing months of dust, and when he pulled a volume free it was with the careless indifference of someone who had no intention of reading it.

"Forget it," he said, not turning around, "It's more boring outside."

A pause. The dust settled in the air between them.

"Besides," the young man said, "you wouldn't actually let me leave."

Eli stood by the fireplace, the light catching the edges of his wings, and watched the young man's back, the dark fall of his hair, the way he held the book like it meant nothing at all.

He did not deny it.

***

The days took on a strange, viscous quality after that, the way they always did when Eli stayed too long away from the Garden.

Eli tried to give the young man a room. He walked in, stood for perhaps ten seconds, then walked back out and lowered himself into the armchair nearest the door. He drew his knees up and did not move again.

"You can stay here," Eli said. His voice carried no surprise, no persuasion. Just the words, offered and left where they fell.

The young man did not answer.

From then on, the armchair became his. During the day he sat with his gaze fixed somewhere past the window. At night, when Eli came down for water, that dark shape remained in exactly the same position, distinguishable from a statue only by the faint rise and fall of fabric.

At set hours Eli would place food on the low table beside the armchair. Bread and stew, or canned meat with rehydrated vegetables. He never called him to eat. Never asked if it suited him. If the food remained untouched after half an hour Eli would clear it away, and if the young man caught him looking, Eli only smiled as though they shared some understanding, even if they did not. The young man ate without pattern. Some nights Eli would find the plate empty, bread torn into crumbs so fine they were almost dust. Other days the food sat untouched until it spoiled.

A week passed. Then, in the dead of night, Eli woke to something that was not quite sound. A shift in the air. The displacement of a body moving through space it had occupied, motionless, for days.

It was past midnight when Eli woke to something that was not quite sound. A shift in the air, a disturbance so slight he could not have named it. He lay still for a moment, listening. Then he rose and moved through the dark until he stood in the living room, ten paces from the empty armchair, and watched.

The young man was at the kitchen counter.

He stood with his back to the room, one hand hovering over the knife rack. The fingers paused there for a long moment, and then drew out the smallest blade. A paring knife. The edge caught what little moonlight came through the window and held it.

Eli did not move. His muscles had drawn tight without his permission, but there was nothing to react to. No killing intent. Just a someone holding a knife the way one might hold a tool whose purpose one had not yet decided.

The young man opened the refrigerator. The light spilled out and wrapped him in its pale glow, and he bent to retrieve an apple from the bottom shelf. He set it on the counter, pressed the blade to the skin, and began to peel.

His movements were slow. Unpracticed. The knife cut too deep in places and too shallow in others, and the peel came away in uneven strips that curled and broke across the counter. He worked with his head bowed, the whole of him wrapped in a kind of silence.

The blade slipped. A thin line opened across his fingertip, white for a moment before it flushed and bled red. He did not pause. Did not wipe the blood away. His hands kept moving as though the body they belonged to was a distant concern, something observed rather than inhabited.

When the apple was finished he held it up and considered it. The flesh was pitted and uneven where he had carved too deep. He bit into it anyway.

He chewed slowly, mechanically, eyes fixed on some point in the empty air. He swallowed and took another bite, and another, until the apple was gone. All of it. Even the core.

He set down the knife. Wiped his palm across his mouth. Closed the refrigerator door and let the darkness take the kitchen back. Then he walked to the armchair and sat down and drew his knees to his chest, and it was as though nothing had happened at all.

Eli watched from the shadows. He thought the night might pass this way, the two of them suspended in their separate silences.

Then the young man's head turned.

Their eyes met in the dark. For a moment neither of them moved. Something passed across the young man's face, quick as a fish beneath black water, there and gone before it could be named. Then his gaze returned to that familiar emptiness, that depth that swallowed everything and gave nothing back. He did not speak. He only looked at Eli the way he might look at a piece of furniture that had shifted slightly from where he remembered it.

Eli looked back. He let his face hold nothing. No curiosity, no sympathy, no accusation. Just calm, as though finding someone eating an apple at midnight was the most ordinary thing in the world.

It was the young man who moved first. He rose and walked past Eli without a word, his steps quick and hard, carrying him down the corridor toward the room he had refused. The door closed behind him with a sound so soft it was barely a sound at all.

Eli stood alone in the dark for a long moment. A weariness had settled into him, not the kind that sleep could fix. Eli shut his eyes.

The young man had chosen the apple. Of all the food he had ignored, he had risen in the dark and chosen this. He had taken the knife. Peeled the skin. Eaten.

Eli did not let himself think past that.

In the morning he placed a fresh apple on the low table beside the armchair. Next to it, he set the paring knife.

The young man glanced at them when he emerged. His eyes rested on the apple, then the knife, then moved away. He sat down and did not reach for either.

That afternoon, when Eli returned, the apple was gone.

The knife remained where he had placed it. The blade had been wiped clean. It caught the grey light from the window and held it, bright and cold.

Eli picked it up. His thumb moved along the edge.

There was no blood.

***

Morning light squeezed through the gap in the curtains and cut a thin bright line across the carpet.

Then the alarm went off. The sound drilled out of the walls, rose up through the floorboards, and flooded the room. Footsteps pounded down the corridor. Someone was shouting, but the words were impossible to make out.

Eli jolted awake.

"Citadel has breached the lower barrier. " Rae's voice sounded in his mind, slightly quiet under the cacophony.

Eli was already at the window. He pushed it open and cold morning air rushed in. The alarm spilled out into the sky and was answered by others rising across the building.

He glanced back. Across the room, in the armchair, the young man sat with his back to him. He had not moved. He might have been asleep. He might have been awake. With him, there was never a way to tell.

Eli pulled his gaze away.

"Eli." Rae paused. "Be careful. I don't know what they're here for."

"I think I do," and then he was gone. His wings threw the curtains to the ceiling. They hung there, fell, and the line of light on the carpet resumed.

The alarm fell silent. Quiet reclaimed the room, and held it for perhaps three minutes, perhaps five.

Then came a faint hiss, like the last sigh of something being burned alive. A thin red line appeared around the door lock. The lock cylinder dropped out of the door and fell onto the carpet without a sound.

The door opened and six people came through it. First barrel sweeping left, second right, third and fourth along the walls, boots on thick carpet, noiseless. The last two dragged a man between them. His feet left shallow tracks. His hands were bound behind him and there was something in his mouth and he was making a noise through it, thin and constant.

The door shut.

Dust turned in the morning light. Six men breathing behind masks.

The squad leader's gaze passed over his men's shoulders and found the armchair.

Someone was sitting in it, with his back to the room. Dark hair. One shoulder touched by the light from the window. He sat very still. Not the coiled stillness of someone who has heard a door open behind him. Just still, the way the furniture was still, the way the walls were still.

The squad leader raised his fist and everyone stopped.

One second. Two.

"Don't move."

The young man did not move.

Then his head began to turn. Slow enough to track the light sliding from the back of his skull to the curve of his ear, from his ear to the plane of his cheek. A quarter turn. He stopped there. That was all he offered them. Just enough for every person in the room to see his expression. Nothing. Nothing at all. His eyes held nothing at all.

"Hands up. Turn around." The squad leader's finger settled on the trigger. "You're coming with us." He kicked the hostage forward and the man crumpled, knees hitting carpet. "If you refuse, we will kill this intelligence officer right here and leave the body at your feet. How well do you think that Auralis General will take your side of the story?"

"You won't shoot him," The young man said simply.

The squad leader's gaze dropped to the hostage at his feet, snapped back to the chair. "You think I won't?"

"Please, go ahead." The young man opened his hands, palms up, and spread them wide. "Exercise your free will as you wish. To me, both outcomes are the same."

His gaze found the hostage. Rested there.

"Though if you do decide to pull the trigger, I would recommend the base of the skull." He raised one hand and touched the back of his own neck, just below where the bone ended. “Three centimeters below the occipital bone. The brainstem meets the cord here. Death will arrive before the pain signal does." He tilted his head, "Consider it a humanitarian suggestion."

The hostage screamed. It came through the gag high and thin and airless. It did not stop.

The squad leader's hand was shaking. *You won't shoot him*, he young man had said. The squad leader's finger found the trigger and pressed past the resistance and he was not sure, even after that instant, whether he was proving the young man wrong or proving him right. He was not sure it mattered at all. But it did not matter anymore now.

The shot hit every surface in the room and came back from all of them at once. Then the ringing. The cordite. The body on the carpet, mouth still open around the gag, eyes wide.

The young man considered the body for a moment.

"Good," he said.

The squad leader's finger was still on the trigger. Sweat crept from his hairline down the side of his face, pooled in the hollow of his collar, ran cold along his spine. The inside of his visor had fogged at the edges and his vision was starting to blur. He blinked. Just once. Just to clear his eyes.

When he opened them, the young man was starting to stand, impossibly slow. They could see his shoulder blades loosen before they drew together, could see his spine straighten one vertebra at a time, could see the hem of his shirt peel away from the seat of the chair and fall and go still.

The young man turned to face them.

Morning light poured in from behind him and swallowed his edges. His face was in shadow. Only his eyes gleamed in the dark.

Every gun barrel in the room dipped three degrees, maybe less. No one decided to do it. No one was aware of doing it. It was the reflexive hunching of an animal that has just understood, instinctively, that it is no longer the largest thing in the room.

"There are six of you," he said. "Two at the door. Two left. Two right. Standard close-quarters formation. Your instructors would have told you that this arrangement offers maximum suppressive coverage in a confined space." He paused. "They may not have mentioned that it has one critical flaw. If someone breaks through the center, your firing lines will cross."

The squad leader's index finger was pressed against the first stage of the trigger. Standard ready position. His instructor had told him it saved a tenth of a second. That tenth of a second had been stretched into something thin and taut and humming, like a wire about to snap.

"I am going to walk through the middle of your formation," the young man said. "The first person to fire will be you." His gaze moved to the man on the right. It settled there, and the man on the right felt it settle, felt it land on him with a weight that had no business belonging to a pair of eyes. "Your index finger is on the first stage. The bend at your knuckle is forty-two degrees. You are fighting the impulse to fire, but you already made this decision before you knew you were making it."

The operative’s breathing turned ragged, like something being dragged over concrete.

"You will fire. The bullet will pass me at this angle." The young man shifted his weight, turning his shoulder slightly. "It will strike your teammate to the left, just below the clavicle. The impact will throw him backward into the coffee table. The glass will tip and shatter. A fragment will hit your squad leader's right wrist. And in that moment..."

He looked at the floor. Considered it.

"Your squad leader's weapon will be in my hand."

He stopped talking.

The room held nothing now but breathing. Heavy, ragged, hissing through mask filters. And heartbeats. Too many heartbeats layered on top of each other, impossible to separate, just a relentless pounding that filled the chest and the throat and the room, a room that had suddenly become very, very small.

Time stretched. Long enough to watch the dust turn in the beam of light from the window. Long enough to hear sweat slide down a forehead and hit the inside of a visor. Long enough to feel every tremor in every finger on every trigger.

The young man stood against the light, and looked at them.

Then he took a step.

The squad leader's finger tightened on the trigger.

A second step.

The operative on the right was making a sound behind his mask, too high and thin to pass as breathing.

A third step.

"Fi—"

Seven, eight shots crashed into each other, impossible to separate, impossible to sequence. The shriek of rounds tearing air. Glass exploding. The heavy wet sound of a body hitting the floor. The coffee table going over, legs snapping, ceramic cups and saucers detonating against tile. A painting blew off the wall and sprayed wood splinters and glass shards across the room like grapeshot. Muzzle flash strobed through the dim room, on, off, on, off. Droplets of blood hung suspended in the air for an instant, lit amber by the flare, then fell. Metal on metal. Bone giving way. Brass casings raining onto carpet, bright and almost cheerful. Gasping. A strangled cry.

And under all of it, through all of it, footsteps. Even. Measured. Steady as a metronome on the carpet, cutting through the noise the way a knife cuts through water.

Three seconds. Maybe four.

Then everything stopped. Smoke drifted and settled.

The operative on the left lay on his back, left hand clamped over his right shoulder. Below the collarbone. The exact place. Blood welling between his fingers. His rifle had landed a meter away. The one with the scratched visor sat propped against the wall with a hole in his chest and his eyes still open behind the cracked lens. The operative on the right, the one who had fired first, lay face down with his neck at an angle that the human neck does not go to. The coffee table was on its side. The glass top had become a thousand glittering pieces across the floor. The painting hung by a single corner of its frame.

The squad leader's wrist was bleeding. A sliver of glass jutted from the soft underside, and blood ran along his forearm and soaked through the edge of his glove and fell, drop by slow drop, onto the carpet. Tap. Tap.

His gun was not in his hand. It was on the floor, half a meter from the young man's feet.

The air smelled of copper and something sickening that turned out to be perfume leaking from a shattered decorative bottle on the shelf.

The young man stood in the center of the room. Every strand of his hair was in place.

Then he bent down. He picked up the squad leader's gun with two fingers on the barrel, the way a person picks up something they have found on the ground and are deciding whether to keep. He looked at it. Then he straightened, and with a flick of his wrist, tossed it.

It landed at the squad leader's feet. The carpet swallowed most of the sound.

"Pick it up."

The squad leader did not move.

"Pick it up," the young man said again, "and kill me."

He raised both hands above his head.

"Shoot."

The squad leader stood there. His legs were shaking. His vision was shaking. The room was the same room it had been thirty seconds ago and it was not the same room at all. He looked at the gun on the carpet. He looked at the young man standing with his hands raised and his face turned toward him and that faint, impossible smile, and the squad leader thought: I am going to die in this room. He did not know where the thought came from. He bent down and picked up the gun. The grip was slick with his own blood. He raised it and aimed at the young man's chest and his hand would not stop trembling.

The young man began to walk toward him.

One step. The squad leader's finger tightened on the trigger.

Two steps. His breathing had gotten away from him. It was ragged inside the mask, too fast, filling his ears.

Three steps. Four.

"The first shot," the young man said, still walking, "you will aim for the heart. But you are firing with your off hand, and the round will pull left. It will sever a superficial vessel. It will bleed. It will not slow me down."

The squad leader fired.

The young man's body shook from the impact. On his black shirt a dark wet shape opened like something blooming. He looked down at it. He touched the fabric with his right hand and lifted his fingers and considered the red on them mildly, and then he kept walking.

"The second shot, you will correct. You will aim for the head. The round will graze my temple. A shallow laceration. Minor concussion. I will be dizzy, but I will remain conscious."

The squad leader fired.

Blood sheeted down the side of the young man's face. It followed the line of his cheekbone, gathered at his jaw, dripped onto his shoulder. He blinked. He did not stop.

Five steps. Six.

One meter between them.

"The third shot." The young man's voice had not changed. It had not changed after the first bullet or the second, as though the two holes in his body were happening to someone else, "You will panic. When a person panics at this range, they aim for the closest lethal target. Destruction of the brainstem. This is, in theory, the fastest way you could kill me right now."

He stopped. He was close enough that the squad leader could see the blood on his temple still running, could see the wet patch on his shirt spreading, but could not feel the warmth coming off him. The young man reached out and took hold of the gun barrel. The metal was still hot. He did not flinch. Slowly, with the careful deliberateness of someone adjusting a picture frame, he drew the barrel toward himself and pressed the muzzle to the center of his own forehead.

"But this shot," his lips formed a thin smile, "you will not take."

The squad leader's knuckle went white on the trigger. Sweat rolled from his forehead into his eyes and he could not blink, could not look away, could not do anything but stand there with the gun pressed to this man's skull and his finger on the trigger and every muscle in his body screaming at him to squeeze and his finger would not move. It would not move. The trigger was at the break point and his finger was on it and the man was standing in front of him with two bullets in his body and blood running down his face and smiling, smiling, and his finger would not move.

The door crashed open.

Eli stood in the doorway. His wings were half-spread, his clothes damp with morning cold, and his eyes moved across the room in the time it takes to draw a single breath. The soldiers on the ground. The blood on the walls. The body of the hostage. And then the young man, standing in the middle of it all, one hand wrapped around the barrel of a gun whose muzzle was pressed to his own forehead.

"Put the gun down," Eli drawled slowly.

The squad leader flinched as if woken from a nightmare.

The trigger broke.

The firing pin struck the primer with a sound like a small bright bell, clear and sharp.

No shot. Just that sound, and then nothing. A hollow click, empty, final, the sound of a chamber with nothing left to give.

The squad leader looked down at the gun in his hand. He stared at it. He stared at it the way a man stares at his own hands after they have done something he cannot take back, except nothing had happened, nothing at all, and that was worse. He looked up. He looked at Eli.

"General." The words that tore out of his throat were almost a scream. "General! You don't know what this is. You don't know who he—"

The shot came from nowhere.

Nobody saw the young man draw. One moment his hands were raised above his head and the next the squad leader had a hole in the center of his forehead. His eyes were still open. His mouth was still open. The second half of his sentence was still in his throat. He went backward and hit the carpet and did not move again.

The room was very quiet.

The young man held the gun at arm's length, the barrel still trailing a thin ribbon of smoke. He stood among the dead and the dying in the morning light, blood drying on his temple, blood spreading through his shirt, and he looked at Eli the way he always looked at Eli, which was to say he looked at him as if Eli were the only thing in any room worth the effort of seeing.

"General," he said.

He let the gun fall.

"Your timing is excellent."

He lifted his foot and stepped over the squad leader's body. His shoe came down in the blood that had pooled around it and the carpet made a soft, wet sound.

"Let me tell you a story," he said as he walked. "About how an intelligence officer died. About why a bullet didn't fire." He paused. That fraction of a curve returned to the corner of his mouth, "About how I came to be standing here."

Eli said nothing.

"When you received the alarm this morning, the lower barrier had been breached. There, You found automated turrets and a looped interference signal. You realized what had happened and you turned back." The young man stopped walking. "But you were late. You were always going to be late. And while you were gone, a full Citadel extraction squad came through your front door."

He looked at Eli.

"Do you know how they got in?"

He took a step forward.

"I gave them the codes."

Another step.

"Do you know why you weren't here?"

Another.

"I designed the false alarm."

He stopped. He was standing in the widest part of the blood now, in the place where several pools had met and merged, and his shoes were dark with it. He glanced down at the hostage's body.

"I killed him too. Do you know why?" He did not wait for an answer. "Because I needed to waste a bullet. I needed one fewer round in that man's magazine. I needed him to pull the trigger at exactly the right moment and hear—"

He raised his right hand. His thumb and forefinger formed a circle. Then, slowly, he opened them.

"Click."

He laughed. It was a quiet sound, barely there, and it did not belong in this room.

Then the young man reached inside his coat with his right hand, the one that was not bleeding, and drew out a sheaf of papers. He held them up. He looked at Eli once.

He threw them into the air.

They rose and turned and caught the morning light, white and flashing, and then they fell. They fell on the carpet. They fell in the blood. They fell on the bodies, on the shattered glass, on the overturned coffee table. They fell at Eli's feet.

Eli looked down. The nearest page had landed face up. It was ordinary paper, unheaded, unmarked, no stamps, no seals, no official insignia of any kind. But it was covered in handwriting so dense and so fast that the letters blurred into each other. Troop deployments. Personnel rosters. Equipment manifests.

He picked it up. He picked up another. Another. His eyes moved across the pages. The handwriting deteriorated as he went, some sections dissolving into scratches that were almost impossible to read, but the information on every page matched what he knew to be true, matched documents that existed only in locked rooms behind locked doors. And in the margins of every page, in red ink, the same annotations repeated.

*They die here.*

*They lose here.*

*Here. Here. Here.*

Eli looked up.

The young man stood in the center of the room. The light from the window came through behind him and lit the pages on the floor and the blood on the floor and the smoke still hanging in the air, and he stood in the middle of all of it, in the middle of everything, and his eyes were fixed on Eli.

"Tactics. Intelligence. Prophecy." His voice was quiet. "Auralis had always been fixated on chasing down the Citadel’s mole."

He paused.

"They didn’t need one, really. They only need me. And I was never present."

He paused again. Longer this time.

"I know everything."

The last page settled into the blood. The paper darkened as it drank.

*"Because I am the Prophet."*

His eyes burned. There was no other word for what they did. They held Eli's gaze and they burned, bright and fixed, the way coals burn after the fire has consumed everything there is to consume and only the heat remains, pure and purposeless and absolute. He did not speak. He did not move. He stood in the middle of a room full of dead men and scattered paper with two bullets in his body and the morning light turning the blood on the carpet to amber, and he waited.

The last page that fell on the floor curled at its edge, where the blood had soaked it.

Eli looked at him.

The silence went on.

It went on long enough for the last of the smoke to settle. Long enough for the blood to stop spreading and begin to darken at its edges. Long enough that the young man's hands, still raised, still open, began to look less like a declaration and more like something held up past its purpose.

"Are you finished?" Eli said.

The young man's face changed. It was fast. A fracture in the surface, there and closed again, but it had been there. His mouth opened a half-second before any sound came out.

"For now," he said.

"Alright."

Eli folded the paper in his hand. Slowly, carefully, matching the edges. Once, then again, pressing the crease with his thumb until he had a small, neat square that he could hold in his hand. Then he walked. He stepped around the blood. He stepped around the bodies. He stopped in front of the young man, one pace away.

"You're not surprised?" the young man asked.

"Would you like me to be?"

Before the young man could force another response, outside the door came the sound of boots, fast, then fists against wood.

"General? General! The door's locked. Are you alright in there? We heard—"

Eli did not look away from the young man.

The hammering doubled. "General! Do we breach?"

Only then did Eli turn and cross the room, through the blood, over the glass, over the pages scattered on the carpet. When his hand found the handle and opened it, outside stood three soldiers. Their swords were aimed at the gap.

"General, you—" One said as they dropped their weapons.

"I'm fine." Eli smiled. "Sorry, the door seems to have jammed. I've handled it."

They stared at him. The boldest one leaned to look past his shoulder into the room. His eyes traveled across the carpet, the blood, the bodies, the dead man's open eyes. And for a moment, he thought he saw another figure, drenched in blood.

Eli shifted half a step. The room vanished behind him.

"You can return to your posts."

"General, but—"

Eli waited. The soldiers exchanged a glance, saluted, turned, and there footsteps soon receded down the corridor.

Eli closed the door.

*Click.*

Eli didn’t turn around right away. He saw from his periphery that the young man had raised his hand absentmindedly and drew his fingers across the wound at his temple. He held them up to the light and examined them, turning his wrist slowly, watching the red darken.

"Tell me something," Eli said. "Have we crossed paths before?"

The young man's hand hung in the air. "Meaning?"

"Meaning exactly that." Eli turned around, his feet stopping just short of the red pool on the ground. "How long have I existed in your famous prophecy?"

The young man looked at him. Then he scoffed. It was a sharp, short sound, a sound meant to reduce the person hearing it, and his chin lifted and his eyes narrowed and for a moment he looked down at Eli the way someone look down at something very far below.

"You think you're that important."

"Yes."

"That will kill you one day," the young man mocked, but the scoff had died out.

"And yet here you are. So what does that make you?"

The young man did not answer. Eli did not press further. So they just stood there.

"What will you offer me to stay?" The young man spoke first. His voice had dropped.

Eli looked at him. For a long time he said nothing at all, and the room waited with him, and the blood went on falling from the young man's body to the carpet in that same unhurried rhythm, and the light went on crawling between them across the floor.

"Nothing," Eli said.

The young man's lashes flickered.

"Because you'll stay." Eli's voice was quiet. "Whether I offer you everything or nothing at all, you will stay. Until your prophecy has nothing left to show you. Until every future has been spent."

His eyes held the young man's. They were blue, those eyes, pale blue with flecks of gold that caught the morning light, and they did not waver. They did not blink. Even as the cold came through.

"You will stay."

He paused.

"Isn't that right?"

The only sound in the room was blood, still falling. *Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The young man's head dropped. His shoulders moved. Once, twice. A sound came from somewhere inside him, low and airless. It could have been a laugh. It could have been something else. It lasted two breaths, maybe three, and then his shoulders were still.

At last he lifted his head. He looked at Eli. Whatever had been on his face a moment ago was gone. Whatever had replaced it, Eli could not read.

He walked. Toward Eli, past Eli, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. Past the blood and the glass and the scattered pages, past the overturned coffee table, past all of it, until he reached the window.

He stopped there. The glass ran floor to ceiling and the morning was still happening on the other side of it, indifferent, ordinary. He stood with his back to the room and its dead and he did not turn around.

"What's your name?"

Eli did not move from where he stood.

The silence lasted long enough that the morning light shifted on the carpet, long enough that the blood on the pages had time to dry a shade darker.

"Luca," he said quietly.

He did not turn around. He stood at the window with his hands at his sides and the city spread below him like something that belonged to someone else, and Eli stood behind him in a room full of bodies.
