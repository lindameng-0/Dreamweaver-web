Eli sat at his desk for another minute. Then he stood, left the tea where it was, and went out through the door this time.

The elevator was too slow. He took the stairs, and by the third landing he could already hear the market. It was the wrong kind of sound. Faster, pitched higher, voices layered over voices with an edge that turned every word into the same word. The drone. The barrier. A hole.

He came out of the Skyscraper's ground-floor entrance and into it.

People were moving. All of them, all at once, pouring through the market square in a current that had no center and no direction. Stalls were half-shuttered. Awnings flapped loose where merchants had abandoned them mid-roll. The fruit seller on the corner had left his scale and his oranges were rolling across the cobblestones and people were stepping on them without noticing and the smell of crushed citrus mixed with dust.

He could see why. Beyond the rooftops, past the residential quarter, the barrier was visible. It was always visible from here. But now there was a hole in it. A dark shape, ragged at its edges, large enough to see from the market square, and through it came the grey light of the wasteland, flat and colorless, pouring through the gap the way cold air pours through a broken window. People were running from it. Some were running toward it. Most were standing still, looking up, looking at each other, waiting for someone to tell them what to do.

Eli moved into the crowd. The threads slipped from his fingers in thin gold filaments that trailed behind him as he walked. They brushed against the people he passed, settling on a shoulder here, a wrist there, and where they touched, the breathing slowed. A woman who had been clutching her bag to her chest with white knuckles loosened her grip. A man who had been shouting at no one stopped shouting. They did not notice. The threads dissolved on contact, leaving nothing behind.

Eli kept walking. He was looking for one person in a crowd of hundreds and the crowd was not cooperating. Even worse was the fact that he had no reason to believe that person was here and every reason to believe that person could be anywhere, could have left through the gate, could have walked into the wasteland through the hole in the barrie.

Then he saw him.

A flash of dark hair between two stalls. There and gone. Eli changed direction. He moved through a knot of people clustered around a water pump and came out the other side and there was Luca again, twenty meters ahead, walking with the unhurried gait of someone who belonged exactly where he was. He turned a corner. Eli followed.

He caught another glimpse between the cloth merchant's hanging fabrics, Luca's profile half-hidden by a bolt of blue linen. He was holding something to his ear. A comm unit, small and dark, pressed close. His lips were moving. Then a group of people crossed between them, four or five of them moving together, and when they passed the comm was gone and Luca was walking again with his hands in his pockets as though he had never stopped. Eli filed it where he filed everything about Luca that he was not yet ready to ask about.

Eli kept his distance. He moved through the market with the easy pace of a man taking a walk, nodding to people who recognized him. Eli passed through them all and kept his eyes on the dark hair that appeared and disappeared among the crowd like something surfacing and diving.

He lost him near the southern wall. The alleys between them were narrow enough that two people couldn't walk side-by-side. Eli stood at the mouth of one and looked down its length and saw nothing. He turned back to the market. Scanned the crowd. The dark hair. The particular way Luca stood.

He found him standing at the edge of a fruit stall, watching the merchant weigh plums on a hanging scale. His hands were at his sides. His posture was the posture of a man with nowhere to be and nothing on his mind. The merchant added a plum to the scale and the brass pan dipped and Luca watched it dip with what appeared to be genuine interest.

Eli walked to the next stall. He bought a pear. He bit into it standing beside Luca, a little closer than Luca would have chosen, and watched the merchant add another plum to the scale.

"It worked," he said, without turning his head. "The reconstruction held. Sylvia is satisfied." He took another bite. The pear was good. Slightly underripe. "She's already pivoting. Planning retaliation against Citadel. As far as the generals are concerned, Citadel lied about the Prophet to justify the drone, and I fought the extraction squad myself."

The merchant adjusted the weight on the scale. Luca watched the needle settle.

Eli chewed. Swallowed. Looked at the pear in his hand, at the place where his teeth had broken the skin, at the pale flesh already browning in the air.

"None of which matters."

He said it the same way he'd said the rest.

"To flood the room in time, I had to draw energy from Rae. The volume I needed, in the window I had." He took another bite. "And Rae can sense every ability user who has ever set foot in this city. Every signature. She has known about you since the night you walked through the gate. An ability user she doesn't recognize, living on my floor, and she has said nothing, and I don't know why."

Luca's gaze stayed on the scale. The merchant had finished weighing and was wrapping the plums in brown paper. Luca watched his hands fold the corners, tuck them under, smooth the seam.

"The generals will never get proof of who I am," Luca said. "Unless I decide to give it to them."

Eli looked at him. "She already knows I lied, Luca. She doesn't need to know who you are. She knows there is someone on my floor whose signature she has never seen before, and she knows I just drained her reservoir to blind a reconstruction team that was coming to examine my quarters." He turned the pear over in his hand. "She has put those together already. She put them together before I lowered my hands."

The merchant handed the wrapped plums to a customer. The customer paid. The scale sat empty.

"The only thing I can do is hope she stays neutral. Rae is kind. But kind and silent are not the same thing, and I have given her very little reason to be either."

They walked back. Eli had expected resistance, an argument, or at the very least one of Luca's pointed silences that meant he was choosing to comply while making it clear that the choice was his. But Luca simply fell into step beside him, matching his pace, his hands back in his pockets, his gaze moving across the market stalls and the shuttered windows and the people still milling in that restless uncertain way.

"Eli!"

He knew the voice before he turned. Mina was coming toward them from the direction of the bakeries, half-running, her apron still on and dusted with flour, her hair escaping from its tie in the way it always did when she'd been working near the ovens.

"Eli, the barrier." She reached them and her hands found his arm and gripped. "I was in the bakery and I heard the sound and I came outside and I could see through it. I could see the grey. I've never seen the grey before, Eli, I've never, it was just there, right there, and people were running and I didn't know what to do, I thought it was falling, I thought the whole thing was coming down—"

Eli reached for her hand. He took it in both of his and held it. "We're safe," he said. "What you saw was a small breach. Rae is already sealing it personally."

"But I saw it from the window, Eli, I saw the sky through it, the actual sky outside, and people were running and I didn't know if I should run or stay or."

"You should stay." He squeezed her hand. "You should go back to the bakery and finish whatever you were making and it will be all right. I promise."

Her breathing slowed. Her grip on his arm loosened. She blinked, and her shoulders came down from wherever they had climbed, and the color returned to her face in a way that seemed to confuse her, as though she couldn't remember what she'd been so afraid of.

"Okay," she said. "Okay." She pushed her hair from her face with both hands and held it there, eyes closed. When she opened them she was steadier. "There's something else."

Eli waited.

"The Garden."Mina’s voice dropped. “Something's wrong with it. I went yesterday, and the flowers were wilting. Right in front of me. I watched them close up and go brown and it happened so fast, Eli, like something was pulling the life out of them." She was speaking quickly now, the words tumbling. "And there are places that are just gone. Paths I used to walk that open onto nothing now. And people. Lia. She just disappeared. When I found her in her home, she kept saying that she couldn’t find her way back anymore."

She looked at him.

"I don't know what's happening. I don't know what to do."

Eli was quiet for a moment. The market noise settled around them, hammers and brooms and the slow resumption of commerce.

"The Garden will be all right," he said, and smiled. "I'll be back as soon as I can. There are things I need to handle here first, but the Garden is not going anywhere. I promise you that."

"You promise?"

"I promise."

She searched his face. Whatever she found there was enough, because she nodded, slowly, and tucked her hair behind her ear and let out a breath. Then her gaze moved past him.

"Oh," she said. "I'm sorry. I didn't even notice you there." The curiosity came back into her face, "I'm Mina. Are you a friend of Eli's?"

Luca had been standing a half step behind Eli's shoulder, close enough to hear, far enough to be peripheral. He inclined his head.

"A recent acquaintance," he said. His voice was pleasant. "It's nice to meet you in person, Mina. Eli’s mentioned you."

Mina's smile widened. "He talks about me? That's either very sweet or very concerning." She laughed.

Then she looked at him a little longer. Her smile stayed in place but something behind it shifted, the way a candle flame shifts when a door opens in another room. She couldn't have said what it was. His eyes, maybe. Something about the way they held her gaze without holding anything else.

She decided he was tired. Anyone would be tired on a morning like this.

"You both look like you haven't eaten," she said, and reached into the bag slung over her shoulder and pulled out two small loaves. "Here. I grabbed these before I left the bakery. They're not my best, but they'll do."

Eli took his. Luca took his and looked at it and turned it over in his hand.

"Thank you," he said. The pleasantness in his voice was flawless.

"Make sure he eats something," Mina said to Luca, pointing at Eli. "He always forgets."

"I don’t forget” Eli called back.

"You forget." She was already backing away, adjusting her bag. "I should get back. The ovens are still on and I left in such a rush I didn't even—" She waved the rest away. "Go. Both of you. Whatever you're working on, go work on it. And Eli." She stopped. "Come back soon. The Garden needs you."

She turned and walked toward the bakery, her loose hair catching the light, and Eli watched her until she turned the corner.

His smile stayed in place for a few more seconds. He stood in the middle of the market with the pear in one hand and Mina's bread in the other, and the smile was still there, and then it was gone. It did not fade. It was simply no longer there.

He turned and walked toward the Skyscraper. Luca fell into step beside him. They passed the stalls reopening, the place where the bolt of blue cloth had been and was no longer.

"We're running out of time," Eli said quietly.

Luca bit into Mina's bread. He chewed. He swallowed. He said nothing, and they kept walking, and the Skyscraper caught the midday sun and threw it back across the rooftops in hard bright lines that cut through the old stone the way the grey from the outside cut into the sky.

They opened the door to Eli's quarters and Rae was sitting at the table.

She had her hands folded. The light from the window fell across the table and across her hands and she sat inside it without seeming to notice. Her back was straight. Her shoulders were level. She did not look up when the door opened.

Eli stopped in the doorway.

Luca did not. He walked past Eli into the room, crossed to the armchair, and sat down. He picked up the book he had left on the armrest. Opened it to the page he had folded. His eyes found the line where he had stopped and continued from there as though nothing in the world had changed since he left it, which, for him, perhaps nothing had.

Eli stayed where he was. He knew how Rae sat. Fingers stacked meant the decision was already made. Fingers laced with the right thumb on top meant it was not, and she was waiting for someone else to make it.

Right thumb on top.

Eli stepped inside. The door closed behind him. The latch found the frame with a sound that seemed to come from a long way off.

"Rae," he said. "How long have you been here?"

Rae did not look at Eli. She looked at Luca.

She looked at him the way someone looks at a word they have been told the meaning of but cannot bring themselves to use in a sentence. Her gaze moved from his hands to his face to the book to the place where his wrist met the cuff of his sleeve. She was searching for something. Eli knew what she was searching for. He had searched for the same thing the night he brought Luca through the gate, and he had found it, and the finding had not made anything easier.

Luca turned a page.

Rae's right hand unfolded from the left. Her fingers opened, palm turning upward on the table, and the lamp on the desk dimmed.

It came back. Then it dimmed again and held, filament at half, and the room shifted. Colors lost a shade. The warmth drew back.

But not evenly. The air between Rae's open hand and the armchair thickened. A faint color bled into it, barely visible, the same purple as the barrier at dusk when the light caught it just so. It hung in the space between them the way dust hangs in a beam of sun, except it moved, drifting from her toward him in a slow steady current. The teacup on the low table beside Luca's elbow rattled once against its saucer.

Eli's fingers found the doorframe behind him.

Luca turned another page.

The pen on Eli's desk rolled toward the edge and stopped. The manuscript on the shelf curled at its corners, every page bending the same direction, toward the armchair. The lamp dropped to a third. The purple in the air deepened, less like light now and more like something with weight. It gathered around the armchair in a haze that softened Luca's edges, that made the book in his hands look like something seen through water. Rae's eyes had a faint purple tint. Her palm was open. Her breathing was even. She did not blink.

Nothing came back from the armchair. The energy poured across the room while Luca sat and read, and his hands were steady and his breathing was steady and his face gave nothing. He turned a page. The page pulled against his fingers, straining toward Rae. He turned it anyway.

Eli let go of the doorframe.

The purple had thickened to something almost opaque around the armchair. Rae's amber brightened. The hum climbed. The saucer under the teacup began to rattle, a thin ceramic chatter, and a hairline crack split the plaster above the bookshelf, and the glass in the window flexed inward with a sound like a held breath. Eli watched the energy flow and vanish to produce nothing, not yet, and Rae go on directing that flow because Rae would not stop until the world told her.

The haze was still building, still deepening, still flowing from Rae's open hand in that same steady current, and there was nowhere for it to go. And a human body could only overload on so much excess energy before it became a thing that killed them.

And if it did not overload. If what was inside Luca woke instead. If the thing that made him the Prophet stirred under the weight of all that energy and opened its eyes in this room, in this building, with every general in Auralis a few floors below, with one right in this room that would witness it and also simultaneously possess the ability to kill him on the spot.

Luca's fingers tightened on the book. A fraction. The smallest contraction, there and gone, and Eli could not tell if it was reaction or reflex.

He did not wait to find out. He raised his hand.

The room fell away. The walls, the window, the morning light, the sound of Luca's breathing, all of it thinned and dissolved until there was nothing left but white. Empty white, the default of a dream with no dreamer, the blank that Eli could shape into anything but chose to leave as it was. Rae stood across from him. Her hands were at her sides now, no longer folded, and the composure she had worn at the table looked different here, where there was nothing to rest it against.

The first thing Rae did was pull.

Eli felt it before he saw it. A draw, sharp and deliberate, dragging energy out of him the way she drew from the grid. The white space shuddered. The edges darkened and the floor beneath him thinned to something he could almost see through, and for one moment the dreamscape threatened to collapse entirely, fold inward, drop them both back into the room with the armchair and the book and the young man who was not supposed to exist.

Eli held it. The effort did not show on his face. He held the dream the way he held everything, easily, completely, as though the white space existed because he preferred it to and would continue existing for as long as his preference held.

"Rae." His voice was quiet. "Stop."

The pull eased. Not all at once. It thinned in stages, like a hand releasing something finger by finger, and when it was gone the white settled back into place and Rae stood across from him breathing harder than she should have been.

"You look tired, Rae," he said. The easy concern of someone who noticed things about you before you noticed them yourself.

Rae's mouth opened and closed once.

"The breach point at the barrier has been resealed," she said at last. "I can sustain the current load."

"I didn't ask about the barrier."

"I know you didn't." She straightened. "But that's what we need to talk about. If Citadel follows through on the ultimatum, I'll have to redistribute the energy draw across every ability user inside the city. Including you."

"You can't pull from me."

"I know what you're going to talk about. The Garden." She said it carefully. "I don't know what it is, Eli. None of us do. You've never explained the mechanics and I have never asked."

"You could have asked."

"It wasn't my place."

Eli did not answer. He let the silence sit between them because silence, handled correctly, could be a gift, a space where the other person could choose to step back from what they could say. He was giving her the chance to stop here. To leave it at the implication.

"Eli.” She hesitated before continuing, “I've known since the day you walked him through the gate."

"I know you have."

She stopped. That was not the answer she had prepared for. Eli could see it in the way her weight shifted, the way her rehearsed next sentence dissolved before it reached her mouth. She had expected denial, or surprise, or at least the performance of surprise. She had built her approach around it. And he had taken the floor out from under her in four words.

"My subordinates inside the tower saw you bring him in," she said, slower now, "And I felt you draw energy from the grid. "

"Yes."

"You flooded the room so the specialist would find nothing."

"Yes."

"And the report you filed is false."

"Rae." His voice was warm. "You already know all of this. You've known it for weeks. Why are we here, starting from the beginning?"

"Because the barrier has a hole in it and I have been standing in front of every general in this city for weeks knowing what I know and I am running out of room."

"I know."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing it looks like you've been running out of room for weeks and haven't told a single person, and the only reason I'm here is because a drone flew through my barrier and told the room for you."

The sharpness surprised both of them. Rae's mouth closed. She looked away. The white space flickered at its edges, grey seeping in like weather.

Eli waited. He counted the seconds in his breathing the way he always did when someone needed time to come back to themselves. He let her have it. He let the flicker settle. And when her breathing had evened out and her hands had stilled and the white had pushed the grey back to the margins, he spoke.

"You're right."

Rae looked at him.

"You're right and I should have told you. I should have come to you first. Before the report, before the reconstruction, before any of it." He took a step toward her. "You are the only person in this building who has been holding all of it together and I let you hold this too, without asking, and that was wrong. I'm sorry."

"Then fix it. Turn him in tonight. Go to Sylvia yourself. The ceasefire holds, the barrier stabilizes, the Garden recovers. The cost is one person."

"One person."

"Yes."

"And what happens to that person?"

"That isn't my concern."

"It's mine."

"Then make it not yours." Her voice was tight. Not angry. Tight the way a rope is tight when it's holding something heavier than it was made for. "You've known him for weeks, Eli. Weeks. You have known this city for four years. You have known me for four years. You built the Garden for these people. Whatever he is, whatever you think he's worth, it cannot be worth the barrier."

"He is worth the barrier."

The words came out quiet. Too quiet for the weight they carried. Rae stared at him.

"You don't mean that."

"I do."

"Then tell me." Her eyes found his. "At least tell me what to do, Eli. You brought him here. You lied for him. You wrote a false report and signed your name to it. You clearly know something I don't. So tell me what I should do."

"Give me time."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have right now." He took a step closer. Not aggressive. The opposite. The way you step closer to someone who is standing at an edge. "Five days. Please. That's all I need. The barrier, the city, the generals and their arguments, all of it rests on you, and you have held it, perfectly, for four years. I am asking you to hold it for five more days."

"Five days. And then what?"

"And then I'll tell them myself."

Rae looked at him for a long time. The dreamscape had settled back to white. The dark at the edges had receded. Her breathing was even. Her hands were still. Everything about her was still, the way she had been still at the table when they walked in, the composure of someone who maintains composure the way other people maintain heartbeats, automatically, at great expense, without the option of stopping.

"Sylvia will come to me," she said. "After the council votes. She'll want to coordinate the strike through the barrier grid. She'll have questions about the eastern wing energy readings. She'll be standing in front of me and she will ask."

She did not say what came next. She did not need to.

"I know," Eli said.

"And you're asking me to—"

"I'm not asking you to lie to her. I would never ask you that." His eyes held hers. Blue, pale, the gold flecks catching light that had no source. "I am asking you to wait. You don't have to do anything, Rae. You don't have to choose. Not today. The weight is mine. Not yours. It was never supposed to be yours."

"You keep saying that," she said. "That it was never supposed to be mine. The barrier. The decisions. All of it." A pause. "Everyone keeps saying that." She looked at the white floor. "I'll give you your five days. But I want you to understand something. I am not doing this because I believe you're right. I'm doing this because I don't know what right looks like anymore and you still seem to. And if you're wrong about this, Eli, if you are wrong, I will not be able to help you. "

"I'm not wrong."

"You sound very sure."

"I am."

“And so is anyone else.”

She looked at him one more time. Whatever she saw there, whatever certainty or performance of certainty Eli offered her across that white expanse, it was enough. Or it was close enough to enough that the difference could be ignored, for now, for five days, for as long as she did not have to answer the question she already knew the answer to.

Rae breathed out. "I wish you the best, Eli."

The quarters returned. Morning light. The smell of cold tea. Eli stood where he had been standing, hand still raised, fingers open against air that no longer needed holding. Rae stood where she had been standing, near the table, her gaze directed toward the armchair.

Luca was in it. He had moved there during the dream, walked past both of them and sat down and opened his book. He was reading. Or appearing to read. His eyes were on the page but they were not moving.

Rae looked at him for a long time. Her hand, the one that had pushed energy into him and found nothing, hung at her side. She drew it back slowly and placed it over her other hand, folding them together at her waist, and the gesture was so careful and so precise that it looked like something rehearsed for an audience that was not there.

She looked at Eli.

She did not say anything else. Whatever had been said in the white space would stay in the white space. She turned and walked to the door and opened it and pulled it shut behind her, and the sound it made was so quiet it barely qualified as a sound at all.

The corridor took her footsteps and carried them away. Even, measured. Growing fainter. Then gone.

Eli stood in the quiet. The air was heavy with what Rae had left behind, energy still pooling in the corners of the room, pressed into the walls, saturating the space the way perfume saturates a room long after the person wearing it has gone. He could feel it against his skin. He would feel it for hours. Until the afternoon light had shifted. Until the thin band of sun that had crossed the carpet when they walked in had moved to the far wall and was climbing toward the bookshelf. Hours. It had been hours since the debrief. The distance between then and now felt longer than hours. It felt like weather.

He crossed to the sofa. Sat down. The cushion gave under his weight and he let himself lean back until his head rested against the wall and his eyes were closed and he was still.

The room was quiet. The building was quiet. From somewhere far below came the faintest suggestion of the city, a sound so small it could have been anything or nothing. The energy Rae had left hung in the air around him and did not fade.

From the armchair, the sound of a page not turning.

Then movement. The book closing. The shift of weight in the armchair. Footsteps, soft on the new carpet, crossing to the low table. A small sound, something being set down on wood.

Eli opened his eyes.

Luca was back in the armchair, settled as though he had never left it. On the table between them sat a deck of Tarot cards. Eli’s cards. He could recognize it from how the box was bent at one corner and the edges were soft, even though he’d never moved it from the drawer in months. Eli looked at it. He looked at Luca. Luca was looking at the bookshelf.

Eli sat up.

The energy Rae had left in the room was still there, faint now, thinning at the edges. He picked up the deck and opened the box and let the cards slide into his palm, and then he shuffled them once, badly, the way someone shuffles who learned from watching other people. A few cards bent. He shuffled again.

"Do you know this game?" Eli asked, laying the first row of cards face-down between them.

Eli looked at him over the top of the deck. Luca's face offered nothing. It never offered anything. But he must’ve gone through all the drawers to find this deck of cards somehow, and he had placed them on the table in front of a man lying on a sofa with his eyes closed, and that sequence of actions, ordinary as it was, was the most Luca had ever given voluntarily.

Eli kept laying cards.

The game was a simple one at its heart. You laid the grid all face-down. You took turns flipping cards. Each card defined where you could take the next action. The point was to predict the final card, the one that every other card had been quietly funneling you toward. You named it whenever you were ready, and then played on, and either the board proved you right or it didn't.

Eli explained none of this. He laid the grid and turned the first card and waited, and Luca reached forward and turned the second, and that was enough. They played in silence. The sound they made was thin and papery. The light from the window moved across the floor between them. Eli watched the grid take shape the way a conversation takes shape, each card narrowing what could come next.

They were fourteen cards into the grid when Eli realized he had already lost.

He could not have said when. The spread between them looked open, full of possibility, but he was sure that every card he could reach would lead to the same end, the one that he could not yet see.

"The Hanged Man," Luca said.

Eli turned the center card. The Hanged Man.

He leaned back. Outside, the late afternoon had gone the color of copper, and the light through the window was warmer than it had been in weeks. Someone in the corridor walked past. Voices drifted up from the residential quarter, a woman calling a name, a child answering. Ordinary sounds.

Luca had been out of the room three times that day. Eli knew because he had not been counting, and the number was three. Once to the corridor and back. Once to the window at the end of the hall, where he had stood for eleven minutes looking at something Eli could not determine. Once to the kitchen, from which he had returned with a glass of water he did not drink.

"That's not fair," Eli complained. "You can see the future."

"I didn't."

"Then how?"

"You can’t guess what the last card will be," Luca said, not looking at Eli. He was looking at the cards. "You build toward it. Every move eliminates a possibility and creates a constraint. By the midgame, the final card is already determined. It was determined on the sixth turn. You just can't see it yet because you're still thinking about the card in front of you."

He reached out and rearranged three cards in the center of the grid, moving them back to where they had been eight turns ago.

"Here," he said. "This is where you lost."

Eli studied the grid for a while. Then he smiled and began turning the cards face-down again. "Another round?"

Luca was better. That much became clear in the second game. He barely looked at the grid when he flipped the cards with absent ease. By Eli’s fifth turn, he had stopped trying to win and started trying to understand, tracing Luca's moves backward, looking for the moment the game had been decided. It was always earlier than he thought, somewhere in the opening draws.

But sometimes Eli felt the corridor forming before it closed—whether from watching Luca or the board, he could not say—and played against it. Twice he watched Luca's hand pause over the spread with nowhere to go, recalculating, adjusting, rebuilding his model of the game from the ground up in the space of a few seconds. Eli lost anyway, but it took seven more turns.

The third game went long. Eli pressed toward the center early, cutting off the deeper paths before they could form. Luca adapted, but slowly, thinking now, and when he named the final card—the Tower—Eli rested his fingers on it and said, "Are you sure?"

He turned it over. The Fool.

Luca looked at the card. He looked at it the way he looked at most things, with that flat, depthless attention that gave nothing back. But his hand, resting on the arm of the chair, had curled around the edge of the upholstery, and his thumb was moving back and forth across the worn fabric, a small, repetitive motion he did not seem to be aware of.

Eli reached into his sleeve and held up the Tower between two fingers.

The argument that followed was the longest consecutive stretch of words Luca had voluntarily spoken since arriving in Auralis, and it was about whether replacing the center card before the first flip was a legitimate move or a corruption of the entire premise of the game. It lasted four minutes, and neither of them won it.

They played again. The light through the window deepened from copper to amber to something that had no name, and then the sun was gone and the room filled with the blue of early evening. Eli got up to light the lamp. When he came back Luca had reshuffled the deck and dealt a new grid without waiting.

The sixth game Eli almost won. He felt it this time, the corridor forming, the narrowing of possibilities, and he played against it three moves early. Luca's hand hovered over the spread for longer than it had all evening. When he finally placed his card his fingers stayed on it for a moment, pressing it flat against the table, and Eli watched that small pressure and said nothing.

Luca won anyway. But it was close. Close enough that neither of them mentioned it.

The seventh game they abandoned halfway through when Eli accused Luca of memorizing the shuffle pattern and Luca said that was not cheating, it was paying attention, and Eli said that was exactly what a cheater would say. The lamp threw their shadows long across the ceiling. Someone in the apartment below was playing music, faint enough that it could have been imagined.

Outside, the city quieted in stages. The merchants first, shutters closing, awnings folding. Then the voices from the residential quarter, thinning to one or two and then none. The barrier hummed at the edge of everything, visible from the window as a faint purple line against the dark. Luca did not look at it. He looked at the cards.

They played the eighth game in silence. The ninth. Eli stopped counting after that. The lamp burned low and he did not get up to trim it.

At some point Eli noticed the dead flower on the windowsill. He had not watered it in days. The petals had gone the color of old paper and the stem had bowed under a weight that was no longer there. He looked at it and looked away and Luca, who was studying the grid, said without raising his eyes, "You haven't left this room in three days."

Eli turned a card. "I've been busy."

"You've been here."

"Same thing."

Luca placed his card. The grid was almost full. "Whatever you're neglecting in The Garden to be here, it must not be worth much."

Eli's hand paused over the spread. A fraction of a second, less, and then it continued. He turned the next card. "Nothing in that Garden is going anywhere."

Luca did not press further. He looked at the grid. He looked at the card Eli had just turned. He named the final card and Eli flipped it and he was right, and they sat there in the quiet of the room with the completed grid between them and the lamp almost out.

Midnight had come without announcement. The city outside was dark. The music from downstairs had stopped. The only light was the lamp's last effort and the faint glow of the barrier through the window, purple and constant, holding back whatever was on the other side.

Luca gathered the cards. He squared the deck against the table with a series of small precise taps and set it down between them. His fingers rested on it for a moment. Then he withdrew his hand and leaned back in the armchair and closed his eyes.

He was not sleeping. Eli knew that. But his eyes were closed and his breathing was even and his hands were still and the cards sat on the table in the space between them, squared and waiting.

Eli watched him for a while. Then he reached over and turned the lamp off. The dark came in and filled the room and held it.

He stood. When he reached the hallway he heard the armchair creak, and then footsteps behind him, not following, just moving in the same direction. The soft sound of a door opening.

It closed. Quietly.

Eli stood in the hallway for a moment. Then he went to his own room and shut the door and lay down in the dark.

The dead flower on the windowsill was visible from his bed, a pale shape against the glass. He could just make it out. He closed his eyes.

The morning came grey and without warmth.

Eli dressed in the half-dark of his quarters. He did not turn on the lamp. The armchair was empty. The door was closed. The book Luca had been reading was closed on the armrest, squared neatly against the edge, and the deck of cards sat on the low table where they had left it the night before.

He did not look for Luca. He did not call out. He checked the corridor, found it empty, and left.

Through the corridor, boots on polished stone.

A hundred pairs moving toward the conference level, the mid-morning rush of officers and aides converging on the upper floors. Black leather, grey leather, oxblood, the dull shine of boots polished the night before. They moved in the rhythm of people who had walked this floor a thousand times.

Ahead of them all, one pair moved beneath the sweep of a long cape, white wings folded neatly against the back of it. Ten paces behind, another pair kept time. They matched every turn. Every slowing and quickening. When the cape paused at an elevator they stopped too, angled slightly away, finding the gap between two other pairs that had also stopped.

When the cape moved again, they followed.

Luca did not close the distance. He walked with his hands at his sides and his gaze fixed on the back ten paces ahead of him, an Auralis uniform buttoned to the collar, an empty identification clip at his chest.

The conference level opened into a wider corridor. Some of the current poured into the meeting room itself, filtered through the guards at the doors. A larger group settled along the walls, the officers and aides in pairs and trios, who would wait out here. Luca slowed. He would stand among them. The walls were thin enough, and his hearing was better than most people assumed.

He found a stretch of wall between two aides and leaned against it. From here he could see the meeting room doors, the guards checking identification, the generals filing through. Eli disappeared inside. The doors stayed open.

Luca knew the meeting was about to begin when he heard those footsteps. Heels on tile, sounding from his left. The clicking did not rush as it grew louder, but the aides rushed to straighten their posture.

She came around the corner with her tablet in one hand and her coat catching the grey light the way expensive things do. She did not look at Luca. She looked ahead, at the doors, at the room beyond them, at whatever it was she saw when she looked at a room she was about to enter. Her pace did not change.

Then, as she passed the stretch of wall where Luca stood, she spoke to the air beside him, "I do wish they'd enforce the dress code more carefully. Half the junior staff walking around without their cards." She adjusted her tablet against her hip. "It makes the whole floor look untidy."

Her hand found his. The touch lasted less than a second. Her fingers were cool and dry and they pressed something into his palm. Then She was past him. She was turning the corner. She was gone.

Luca stood very still.

The aides on either side of him had not looked up from their tablets. The officer across the corridor was adjusting his cuffs. No one had seen. He stood against the wall and felt the edges of it dig into his palm.

He did not look down immediately. He stood with his back against the wall and his fist at his side and the corridor moved around. And then he opened his fingers.

An identification card.

His face looked back at him from the laminated surface. His face, from a perfect profile angle he had never sat for, in a perfect light he did not recognize.

His name. His real name. Printed beneath the photograph in clean black type.

An Auralis designation. A division number. An officer rank.

Luca's thumb moved across the surface of the card. He turned it over. The back was blank. He turned it back. The photograph watched him with his own eyes.

Luca looked at the card. He looked at the corridor where Maeve had disappeared. He looked at the meeting room doors, where the guards were checking the last few officers through.

He straightened his collar. Clipped the card to his chest. Walked to the doors.

The guard on the left glanced at the card. His eyes moved from the photograph to Luca's face. He nodded. Luca walked through.

The meeting room was the same room, Except the window on the western wall was new glass that was slightly clearer than the rest, and the scorch mark at the center of the table had been covered with a cloth that did not match.

Generals were taking their seats. Rae at the head, hands folded, ring turning. Emerson with a folder he had not opened. Sylvia at the new window with her arms crossed. Maeve was already seated with her tablet was in front of her. She was adjusting the edge of the cloth with one fingernail, a centimeter to the left, as though that centimeter had been troubling her. She did not look toward the doors.

A chair pulled back. The sound of it was small in the large room. Luca sat down across from Eli.

Luca crossed the room. He pulled back a chair. The legs scraped against the floor and the sound was small and ordinary and no one looked up except one person.

Eli looked up.

Luca sat down across from him. The identification card was clipped to his chest, the photograph facing outward. His hands came to rest on the table.

Eli did not move.

His hand was where it had been, flat on the table. His mouth was where it had been, half-open around a word he had been about to say to the general beside him. The word did not come. His body had understood before his mind caught up, the way a body understands a fall before the ground arrives, and everything in him locked at once. His hand on the table. His breath at the top of his chest. His eyes on Luca's face across four feet of polished wood.

The name on the card. He could read it from here. The photograph. The division number. The fresh lamination catching the light.

Luca looked back at him. The same eyes. The same nothing in them. The same face that had looked at him across a fireplace and across a battlefield and across an armchair in the small hours of the morning, and now it looked at him across a table in a room full of every general in Auralis, and it offered him exactly what it always offered him, which was nothing at all.

Eli's fingers pressed into the wood. The pressure was the only thing moving in his body. His knuckles whitened by a shade. Two shades. His breath would not release. The room was continuing around him, chairs shifting, papers shuffling, the low murmur of people who had not yet been called to order, and all of it was happening at a great distance, on the other side of something he could not name, and on this side there was only the card clipped to Luca's chest and the name on it in black type.

One heartbeat. Two. Three. Four.

Eli breathed. He looked down. His hand relaxed on the table, finger by finger, and the color came back to his knuckles, and he turned to the general beside him and finished the word he had started and the word was nothing, was pleasantry.

He did not look at Luca again.

No one else had noticed. Luca was a face they might have seen in passing, a name on a roster they hadn't memorized, an officer from a division that wasn't theirs. The guards at the door had checked his card and the number was authentic and the photograph matched and there was nothing to see. The room settled around him without disturbance. The doors closed.

"Citadel has given us forty-eight hours." Maeve opened the meeting without preamble, "The deadline approaches. We have two paths to consider, and I would like us to consider them before we commit to one."

She did not look up from the tablet as she spoke. Her nail tapped it once, twice, the way another person might tap a pen against a page.

"The first path. The Prophet is inside Auralis. Citadel's claim is true. In which case our task is to locate him and determine what to do with him before the ultimatum expires."

A small pause. She let the word *him* sit long enough to draw no attention.

"The second path. Citadel fabricated the claim. The extraction team was sent for some other purpose, and the ultimatum is a pressure tactic, designed to make us turn inward while they prepare a strike we cannot see. In which case our task is to prepare a response."

She looked up.

"The reconstruction of General Eli's quarters produced readings that did not contradict his account. That is where we begin."

She folded her hands. She looked at no one in particular.

"I'd like the room to settle on a path before committing resources. The floor is open."

She set her hand flat on the tablet and waited.

The silence that followed was the silence of a room that had been expecting her to argue one way or the other and had been given neither. Eli watched the generals recalibrate in their chairs. Emerson turned to a page in his folder that he had not looked at before. One of the junior generals leaned toward the other and said something too quiet to hear.

Sylvia pushed off the window.

She did not wait for the floor. She was speaking before her hand left the sill, and by the time she reached the table her voice had already filled the room.

"We don't have two paths. We have one. The reconstruction settled it. The specialist confirmed Eli's account. Citadel lied."

"The specialist said the readings did not contradict his account," Maeve said. "That is a different statement."

"It's the same statement." Sylvia did not look at her. Her eyes were on the cloth at the center of the table. "We spend our lives parsing the difference and we'll be parsing it when the barrier comes down. The reconstruction confirmed what we already knew. Eli fought the squad. Eli killed the squad. There was no Prophet. Citadel lied, and then Citadel flew a bomb into our meeting room to keep us from looking at the lie too closely."

Her hand found the cloth. She pulled it off the table in one motion.

The scorch mark underneath was black and deep. The wood around its edges had warped. Someone had tried to sand the worst of it and the sanding had only made the rest of the table look worse by contrast, the polish smooth and intact everywhere except this one place where it had been burned through.

The cloth dropped to the floor.

"This," Sylvia said. "This is what four years of patience bought us. This is what the Metropolis thinks we are. They did not send an army because an army would have been an acknowledgment. This drone is nothing but a blatant dismissal."

Her voice had not risen. The absence of rising was the point.

"And we are sitting here asking whether the message was sincere. Whether Citadel is lying about the Prophet. Whether we should commit to a path." She looked at Maeve for the first time. "While the wall comes down."

"Sylvia." Maeve's voice was gentle. "What are you proposing?"

"I am proposing what I have proposed for four years." She turned back to the room. "We hit them first. We hit the technology that made that drone. The device that breached the barrier exists, and it exists somewhere, and every hour we spend in this room debating paths is an hour they spend building the next one. The Prophet is a distraction. A story Citadel told to make us fight ourselves. We should not be discussing it. We should be discussing targets."

Emerson spoke from the end of the table. "We don't know where the facility is."

"We find it."

"We have looked for four years."

"We have looked while keeping the barrier up and pretending we were not at war. We look now with the understanding that we are." Sylvia's hand closed on the edge of the table. "The deadline is forty-eight hours. They are not giving us time to find the Prophet. They are giving themselves time to finish what they started. We have forty-eight hours before the barrier is irrelevant. Forty-eight hours."

She stopped. The silence she left was the kind that pulled people toward it.

Maeve let it sit. She watched Sylvia without expression. Then she drew a small breath, the sound of someone about to speak gently to a person who was not going to enjoy what was said.

"You've built the case well, Sylvia." She touched the edge of her tablet with one fingernail. "But you've built it on one assumption. The reconstruction did not confirm General Eli's account. It produced readings that did not contradict it. Those are different things."

Sylvia's jaw tightened.

"I know you don't want them to be different things." Maeve's voice was warm. Almost sympathetic. "I don't particularly want them to be, either. But the specialist's own phrasing was 'does not contradict.' Not 'confirms.' The energy density in the room obscured the underlying movement traces. Which means we have a reading that is consistent with the report. We do not have proof that the report is true."

"It's enough."

"For what? For an operation that empties our military sector, crosses the barrier, and depends on intelligence we cannot verify?" Maeve did not raise her voice. She did not need to. "If you're right, we lose nothing by taking an additional hour to confirm it. If you're wrong, we have committed our strongest forces to a strike based on a false premise, and we have left Auralis defended by whatever we could spare."

Sylvia's hand curled on the back of the chair.

"Then verify it."

"I intend to." Maeve turned her head. "Rae."

Rae looked up.

She had been sitting as she always sat, hands folded, ring catching the light, her gaze resting somewhere that was not quite the table and not quite the wall behind it. When her name reached her she did not startle. Her face did not change. But her fingers, the ones that had been laced together in front of her, loosened and then tightened again, and Eli, who had been watching the cloth on the floor, looked up.

"The reconstruction could not read through the energy saturation in the quarters. That is on record. The specialist was clear." Her voice was still level. Still reporting the weather. "But let’s assume the Prophet is an ability user. If there were an unknown ability user inside the barrier, anywhere in Auralis, you would know."

Maeve's gaze had not left Rae.

"I would like the room's doubt settled. So that we can move forward." A pause. "Rae. Is there anyone inside Auralis who should not be here?"

The room turned.

Every general. Eli watched his own head turn with the rest of them, because not turning was no longer an option. His gaze found Rae at the head of the table. Her hands were folded in front of her. Her ring caught the grey light. Her eyes were on her hands.

She did not speak.

The silence was small at first. Small enough that it could have been thought. Small enough that someone who had not been watching Rae specifically might have mistaken it for the pause before an answer. But Eli was watching. So was Sylvia. So was Maeve.

The silence grew.

Rae's thumb touched the band of her ring. Turned it. Once. The smallest motion, almost not a motion at all, the kind of gesture a person made without knowing they had made it.

One of the junior generals whispered something to the other. A chair scraped. Maeve's hand lifted off the tablet. She folded it over her other hand.

"Thank you, Rae," she said.

Rae's hands unfolded. She placed them flat on the table, palms down, the way a person places their hands when they are about to stand. She did not stand. Her ring pressed against the wood.

The water in the carafe at the center of the table had gone still. Eli could see it from where he sat. Perfectly still. The surface held the reflection of the ceiling and the faces around the table, and none of it moved.

Sylvia turned her head.

She did not turn her body. Just her head, slowly, with the terrible patience of something winding itself up. Her eyes found Eli and stayed.

"Get up," she said.

Eli did not get up.

"Get up, Eli. I want to see your face when I ask you this."

He rose. His chair made no sound. He stood with his hands at his sides and his wings folded close and he looked at her across the length of the table, past Rae who had still not raised her eyes, past Maeve whose hands had still not moved.

"I stood in this room three days ago," Sylvia said. "I held a piece of that drone in my hand. I told you that if your account held, I would lead the strike myself. I told you that if it didn't, I would take it out of you personally. Do you remember that."

"I remember."

"I accepted the reconstruction. I stood down. I began planning a response to an enemy that lied about the Prophet. I was wrong about what the enemy lied about. Wasn't I."

Eli said nothing.

"Wasn't I."

"Sylvia." His voice was quiet. "Let's talk about this somewhere else."

Where I dropped an investigation because you told me I could trust the evidence." Her voice had still not risen. "Four years, Eli. I have stood next to you in this room for four years. I have argued with you and I have voted against you and I have thought you were naive and I have thought you were soft and I have thought a great many things about you, and I have never once thought you were a liar. Not once. Do you understand what you have taken from me."

"Don't."

"Does it matter how it arrived?"

"Don't do that, Eli."

"You have your war. Take it."

Sylvia's hand closed around something in the air between them and wrenched downward.

The snap filled the room. Generals flinched. Heads turned. In Sylvia's fist, caught in the space between one second and the next, something thin and gold was already dying, and Eli's hand was still raised, still open, still held in that familiar gesture that every person in this room had seen a thousand times before.

Sylvia held her fist in midair. Her knuckles were white.

She looked at Eli.

"You can use that on the rest of them." Her voice was barely above a whisper. "You've probably been using it on the rest of them for years."

"Good thing I never trusted you," she said.

He lowered them. The motion was slow. He placed both hands on the table and the wood was cool under his fingers.

The scorch mark on the wood sat between them all, uncovered, and no one moved to pick up the cloth.

Every head turned.

Luca was on his feet. His hands came together again, and again, and for a moment no one could place what they were hearing. Clapping. Someone was clapping. A junior officer no one recognized was standing at the table and bringing his palms together with the slow, deliberate rhythm of a man applauding a performance he had thoroughly enjoyed.

He stopped. He looked at Sylvia.

"Brilliant," he said. "Really. All of it. Every word."

Sylvia's hand went to the blade at her hip.

"Don't bother." Luca stepped away from his chair. "I'm agreeing with you. The report was false. The reconstruction was sabotaged. Everything you suspected was correct, General. Congratulations."

"General Eli wrote the report himself. His handwriting. His pen. His very earnest attempt at deception." The corner of his mouth moved. "I had to correct one word. After all, words can get people killed." He glanced at Eli. "It’s surprising how he still doesn't know that."

He continued walking.

"He sabotaged the reconstruction because I told him to. He left for the alarm because I designed it. He has never once, since the night I walked through his door, done a single thing I did not anticipate. He is, in this respect, no different from anyone else at this table."

His gaze moved across the room. General to general. Face to face. It landed on Sylvia. His eyes moved over her with the flat appraisal of someone cataloguing objects in a room.

"General Sylvia, you wanted Citadel to be liars. You wanted it more than you have ever wanted anything in your life, which is a reasonable statement given your history, or rather, everyone’s history. His head tilted.

"I could have said anything on that channel. I could have told you the sky was green. You would have believed it, because it was what you needed, and need makes fools of everyone. Even you."

Sylvia's knuckles were white on the blade. The room had gone very still.

"It is not personal," he said. "Every person in this room performed exactly as expected. That is not an insult. Predictability is what makes the world functional. It is what makes people trustworthy and kind and brave and all the other things that generals are supposed to be."

“Who are you.”

He stopped walking. He stood at the head of the table. “I think you already know who I am. But allow me to reintroduce myself.”

Rae was three feet to his left. He did not look at her.

"My name is Luca," he said. "I am the Prophet."

Grey light on wood. Twelve faces. A table between them. The word Prophet suspended above it all, weightless, not yet fallen.

It fell.

Glass first. Someone's elbow caught a tumbler going for a weapon and it shattered against the floor and that was the end of the room they had been sitting in. Chairs went over. Steel scraped free of sheaths. The comms panel shrieked from the wall where a palm had struck it and the shriek layered over voices, five, six, seven, all of them shouting and none of them saying words anymore, just sound, just the raw noise of twelve people discovering at the same instant that the ground beneath them had never been ground at all. A fist hit the table. Tactical pins jumped from their slots and scattered and rang across the wood like small bright bones.

Sylvia was already past the first general.

She moved the way Eli had seen her move in the field, right after the cataclysm, when a Citadel guard at a Metropolis checkpoint leveled his rifle at a civilian and Sylvia crossed forty meters of open ground before he could chamber a second round. No thought. No decision. Just the body doing what the body was built for, fast and clean and terminal. Her blade was out and her arm was back and her face had left her face, with no hesitation and no thought and no possibility of turning back. Three strides. Two.

Eli's hand came up on reflex. Gold threads left his fingers and it was too slow. He was too slow.

One stride.

The black came from the back of Luca's skull.

It came forward across his hair like ink tipped into water. Over his temples. Down across his eyes. A strip of something that had no name in the light of this room, wrapping itself across his face the way a hand closes over a mouth, settling into place as if tied by fingers that did not exist.

Sylvia's blade stopped.

Her arm stopped. Her legs stopped. Her lungs stopped. The shout that had been building in the chest of the general behind her stopped in his throat like a swallowed stone. Emerson's hand was on the comms panel and it was still on the comms panel. A chair that had been tipping finished its fall and struck the floor and the sound was so loud in the new silence that it could have been a gunshot, could have been anything, because there was nothing else.

Eli's threads hung between his open palms and the space they had been reaching for. Gold. Motionless. He could see them. He could not pull them back. He could not send them forward. His fingers would not close. His lungs would not draw.

Twelve people in a room and not one of them could close their fingers. Not one of them could turn their head or drop their gaze or take the breath their chest was screaming for. Eli's eyes could move. That was all. He could see Sylvia's blade, still raised, still catching the grey light along its edge. He could see the vein in her wrist, rigid, her pulse visible and trapped there, beating against a body that would not answer it. He could see the junior general's mouth, open, the shape of a word frozen in it that would never arrive.

A drop of sweat gathered along Sylvia's jaw. It hung there. It fell.

The black pulled back.

It slid from his eyes across his temples, gathering itself over his hair, collecting into the base of his skull and gone. Luca's eyes opened.

And Sylvia's arm completed its arc.

The blade came down. It came down with the full weight of a body that had been held in place and released, momentum preserved, angle preserved, and it would have opened Luca's throat from ear to collarbone.

A sound. Small. Bright. Metal on metal, high and thin, the sound a coin makes when it strikes a glass.

The blade kicked left. Half an inch. Less. The edge passed so close to Luca's neck that a strand of dark hair fell from it, turning once in the air before it reached the floor. Sylvia's arm carried through and she stumbled forward one step, two, catching herself on the table's edge with her free hand.

She looked down.

A hatpin. Lodged in the flat of the blade near the guard, angled from behind, driven with a precision that did not belong to a object thrown by someone off pure reaction.

Sylvia turned.

Maeve stood at the far end of the table. Her hand was still raised, fingers parted, the gesture of someone who has just released a very small thing into the air. She might have been reaching for a glass of water.

She lowered her hand.

"Sit down, Sylvia."

Sylvia's chest was heaving. Her knuckles were white around the hilt still buried in the table. She looked at Maeve. She looked at the hatpin. She looked at the hair on the floor, that single dark strand, and her mouth opened and nothing came out of it.

Luca stood at the head of the table with his hands at his sides. He looked at the strand of his own hair on the floor. Then he looked up, and his gaze passed over Sylvia and her trembling hands and her blade, passed over Maeve and her lowered hand, passed over all of them, and found Eli.

"If the array is completed, your barrier deactivates. All of it, all at once." He placed a third pin at the center of the map, gently, the way you place a period at the end of a sentence. "And then nothing will happen."

He let that sit.

"For a while, nothing at all. You will stand in this room and look out that window and the sky will be more or less the same sky." He tilted his head. "And then the Metropolis will notice that the barrier is gone. And everything they have been keeping behind it, every weapon, every piece of ordnance they have built and stockpiled and aimed at this city for four years, will come raining down."

The room was very still.

"I can guide an operation to destroy the facility before that happens."

"No."

Sylvia. Flat. Final.

"No. You defected. You. The Prophet. The man who has not lost a single engagement in four years suddenly walks into Auralis and offers us a war.” Her hands were flat on the table. “You think I’m stupid. I’m not stupid. Citadel sent you here to do exactly this, or something happened that was bad enough to make you run. Which is it."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because if Citadel sent you, then every word out of your mouth is designed to get our soldiers outside this barrier. And if you ran, then Citadel is hunting the most valuable asset they've ever had, and you are using us as a shield, and every word out of your mouth is designed to keep us standing between you and them. Either way, we're the ones who die.” she scoffed. “Kill him. Send the body. Buy time. Find the facility ourselves."

"You won't find it," Luca said.

"We pushed the border back ourselves. We broke the Citadel line without your prophecy, without your intelligence, without a single word from you."

Luca's head tilted.

"Yes. You pushed the border. And for four days you held it, and you celebrated, and you believed it meant something." He looked at her. "Who do you think pulled the line back for you to push? Who do you think told Jax to retreat?"

Sylvia's face went blank.

"Every inch of ground you have ever gained was ground I gave you. You did not break the Citadel line, General. I opened it. And I can tell you the exact day I decided to, and the exact reason, and the exact number of Citadel soldiers I let die to make it look convincing. You see, a city that believes that it is winning does not look too closely at why."

Maeve's thumbnail traced the edge of the table. Once. Twice. She was looking at Luca. Not at his face. At his hands. At the way they rested on the table, loose and open, fingers slightly apart.

"You've been planning this." Maeve stated flatly. "The defection. The papers. The reveal. All of it. For how long?"

"Long enough."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one you'll get."

Maeve studied him. The silence between them stretched and Eli watched her eyes move across Luca's face the way her eyes moved across a contract.

At last, she said, "What do you want?"

There it was. The question underneath all the others.

"Asylum," Luca said. "Permanently. Considering that I’m a wanted criminal."

"You want protection." Sylvia spat the word. "From us."

Luca looked at her. "You have five days before Citadel brings down the barrier. If I am dead, you have nothing. If I am alive and the facility is destroyed, Citadel loses their weapon and you gain a prophet." That fraction of a curve at the corner of his mouth. "It's not a difficult calculation."

"It's extortion."

"It's leverage. I understand the confusion."

Sylvia lunged. With a sharp look from Maeve, Rae moved for the first time to catch her arm. Sylvia turned on Rae and Rae did not let go.

"Enough." Maeve set her tablet down. She stood. She smoothed the front of her coat. She walked to the head of the table and stopped there, and when she turned to face the room she was between Luca and the rest of them.

"I do not trust you." She said this to Luca the way she might tell a dinner guest that the soup was cold. "I do not believe you have switched allegiances out of conscience, and I do not believe you are telling us everything, and I am not interested in hearing you pretend otherwise."

She paused. Her fingers found the button of her coat and fastened it.

"However."

The word hung.

"Citadel has given us five days. I do not intend to spend them executing the only person in this room who knows what is coming." She looked at Sylvia. "I also do not intend to hand our military operations to a man who has spent four years ensuring they fail."

She turned back to Luca.

"You will accompany the operation. You will guide the team to the facility. You will not command." She did not wait for his reaction. She was already turning to the room. "I will lead the operation personally. General Eli will manage the Prophet's cooperation throughout. General Rae will maintain a connection to both General Eli and the Prophet through her ability for the full duration of the mission." She paused. "General Sylvia will command the defense of Auralis in my absence."

One second. Two.

Sylvia's chair hit the wall behind her.

"No." She was on her feet and the word was already too small for what was behind it. "This is mine, Maeve. My investigation. My instincts. I told this room the report was wrong. I told you, and you stopped me. I was right. I was right from the beginning and now you're asking me to sit behind a barrier and wait while you walk into the field with him?"

She pointed at Luca. Her hand was shaking.

"I should be the one standing over him when that facility burns."

Maeve waited. She waited until Sylvia's breathing slowed by a fraction, until the hand lowered by an inch.

"You're right," Maeve said. "Your instincts were right. You saw what the rest of us missed. That is precisely why you are needed here." She stepped toward Sylvia. "The barrier is compromised. Citadel's deadline has not changed. If this operation takes longer than we expect, if Citadel moves while our forces are divided, this city will need its strongest combat general on its walls." She held Sylvia's gaze. "There is no one else I would trust with that."

Sylvia looked at Maeve. She looked at Eli. She looked at Luca, who was sitting in his chair with his ankle over his knee, examining a piece of glass dust on his sleeve with focused disinterest.

She sat down. The chair took her weight like a blow. Her hands gripped the armrests and her spine was rigid and her jaw was set so tight that Eli could see the muscles working beneath the skin.

Maeve turned back to the room.

"If at any point the intelligence proves false, Rae will terminate the Prophet immediately. Remotely. No warning."

She let that settle.

"And General Eli." She added

The room turned toward him. Eli felt it. Twelve gazes and the weight of every one of them.

"If at any point General Eli acts to protect the Prophet over the mission, Rae will terminate him as well."

Rae looked up, startled. She almost let go of Sylvia.

"General Rae's connection extends to every ability user in Auralis. Inside the walls and beyond them. There is no distance at which either of them is beyond her reach."

Maeve paused. Her thumbnail found the edge of her tablet case and pressed it once. She turned to Sylvia.

"And in the event that Rae cannot reach them." She looked at Sylvia. Direct and without ornament. "You can do it. Can't you."

"I didn’t need you to ask.”

The room was very quiet. Maeve held it there. She let it stay quiet long enough for every person at the table to feel the shape of what had been built around them, the clean geometry of it, every betrayal accounted for, every exit sealed.

Sylvia leaned forward. Her hands were still on the armrests. Her knuckles had not loosened.

"There is no scenario in which betrayal succeeds." Maeve looked around the table. The decision had already been made. Their silence would be taken as consent. This was the kindness she was offering, the chance to say nothing rather than the obligation to say yes.

One by one, they said nothing.

Maeve looked at Sylvia.

"Is that acceptable?"

Sylvia's arm was still in Rae's grip. Her jaw was working. The vein at her temple was visible and beating.

"If one detail is wrong," Sylvia said. "One."

"One."

Sylvia wrenched her arm free from Rae. She sheathed her blade. The sound was ugly.

"Well then. Meeting adjourned." Maeve said as she walked to the door. "Get some rest before dawn."

The door closed behind her.

Generals rose. They gathered what was left of the morning they had walked into and they left. They filed past Luca's chair one at a time and every one of them looked at him and every one of them looked away and the looking away was different each time. Some fast. Some slow. One junior general held his gaze for three full seconds and then dropped his eyes to the floor and kept them there all the way to the door.

Rae was the last. She paused beside Eli's chair.

The door shut.

Then Luca looked at Eli, murmured something he couldn’t quite catch, and left.
